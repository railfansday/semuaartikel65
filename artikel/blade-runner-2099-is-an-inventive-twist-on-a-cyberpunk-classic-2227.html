Industry observers note that in an age of streaming shows designed to appeal to distracted viewers, Blade Runner 2099 can feel like a welcome anachronism. The Amazon series is slow and methodical, just like the movies it follows, as it delves deep into moral ambiguity. It is very Blade Runner, in that it both feels true to the films but also expands on them in novel ways, showing you sides of this universe that have only been hinted at before. It’s just a little too long for its own good.

Like the movies, 2099 uses the lens of a detective story to explore a much bigger narrative about what it means to be human. The show takes place 50 years after the last film, and a lot has changed. The Blade Runner series is set in a future where humans have developed artificial life-forms called replicants that are nearly indistinguishable from people aside from a few key traits, like being stronger and having a limited lifespan. This made them ideal for working in harsh environments, but they also became second-class citizens, many of whom were willing to fight for whatever kind of life they could, no matter how short.

In between the events of 2049 and 2099 there was a war between humans and replicants, which flipped the dynamic: Now the replicants are in charge, with humans cataloged and relegated to manual labor. The titular blade runners, a kind of special detective force, are now replicants hunting down any humans who go against this fresh order. This sets up a curious dynamic for the show, which centers on two blade runner partners. Olwen (Michelle Yeoh) is a replicant with only a week left to live, spending her remaining time hunting down her missing ex-partner. Soon she’s joined by Cora (Hunter Schafer), a human fugitive posing as a replicant in a ploy to earn freedom for herself and her sick brother.

This premise allows the show to explore the universe from a few upcoming angles. For one thing, the ambiguity over the differences between humans and replicants becomes even cloudier with the roles reversed. Despite having longer lives and the benefit of an actual childhood (replicants are grown in a lab and enter the world as full-grown adults), people in this time are mostly emotional husks, forced to become subservient just to survive. That’s the main reason Cora is able to successfully go undercover. Her rough upbringing hardened her emotionally, making her more capable of pretending to be a replicant — a being who does not experience emotions like empathy or embarrassment.

In a recent development, as blade running partners, the two are pulled across retrofuturistic Los Angeles with very different goals in mind, but they ultimately intersect as Olwen searches for her partner and Cora searches for freedom. I won’t spoil too much, but there’s a MacGuffin that comes into play that has existential ramifications for replicant and human alike, and both detectives are ultimately pulled into its orbit.

In a recent development, exploration of this premise expands the universe of Blade Runner. Some developments are largely implied — you don’t actually see the war, only its aftermath, and there are rumors of a Mexico City that remains a safe haven for humans — but others are shown in exquisite detail. There are parlors where replicants can live out different lives using disturbingly realistic bodysuits, and dingy, underground dance clubs where humans let loose away from replicant eyes. It helps that 2099 looks incredible, evoking the scuffed-up cyberpunk future of the films through chunky computers and intimidatingly brutalist architecture. And yes, both Olwen and Cora wear extremely cool jackets, like all blade runners should.

The main issue is that the series feels like it stretches itself too thin at times, particularly during the middle episodes, which can sag with unnecessary storylines and characters that are underdeveloped. This is exacerbated by the slow-burn structure, which works well for a film but can feel a bit much across eight episodes. Potentially fascinating characters, like a delightfully deranged replicant played by Amy Lennox, feel disappointingly flat. 2099 tries to fill up some of its time by being more action-focused compared to the films, but these scenes often feel at odds with the rest of the show. And as much as I love tense sequences, like the Voight-Kampff test where characters stare each other down like gunslingers in a Western waiting for someone to make a wrong move, this happens a few too many times during the show, diluting the scenes’ impact.

It has been revealed that perhaps the best thing about Blade Runner 2099 is that it ends at all. In an age when franchises are frequently bled dry, 2099 is a relatively brief and welcome dip back into a beloved universe, one with a very final finale. It’s an ending that, much like the original film, opens up all kinds of latest questions — ones that may never be answered.

Blade Runner 2099 starts streaming on Amazon Prime Video on November 25th.
