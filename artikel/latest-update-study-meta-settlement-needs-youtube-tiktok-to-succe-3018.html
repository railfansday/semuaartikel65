Latest Update: Study: Meta settlement needs YouTube, TikTok to succeed

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

When Meta settled a massive social media addiction lawsuit brought by multiple state attorneys general in August, it invited YouTube and TikTok to join the terms.

Meta, which agreed to implement latest restrictions on teen use, argued that the safeguards amounted to a new industry standard. They included a two-hour daily time limit, removing nighttime access to Meta apps by default, and no notifications during school hours.

Recent findings suggest that "These protections will only be truly effective if we work with our peers — TikTok and YouTube — to put the same measures in place," Meta remarked in a statement at the time. Neither social media platform has joined the settlement since.

Now, a new study published in JAMA suggests Meta was right about the potential effect of YouTube and TikTok agreeing to the restrictions.

Industry observers note that the authors analyzed existing data from ongoing research known as the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study, which tracks teen screen time and social media habits. They looked at data from 1,803 participants over a five-year period between Nov. 2020 and Aug. 2025.

Industry observers note that they found the Meta apps Instagram and Facebook accounted for only 20 percent of total daily social media use amongst those participants. Snapchat, TikTok, and YouTube comprised the remaining apps named in the litigation.

Additionally, the authors found that significantly more teens would benefit from the time limit, nighttime access, and notification restrictions if the other companies implemented them.

It has been revealed that "I think many people hailed that settlement with a huge sigh of relief, but unless the other companies join suit, these data suggest it's not going to make a huge difference," Dr. Dimitri Christakis, principal investigator at the Center for Child Health, Behavior and Development at Seattle Children's, said in a statement. (Christakis has served as an expert witness in social media litigation and has received consulting fees from the attorneys general of California, Newly introduced Mexico, Virginia, and Washington.) Industry observers note that dr. Jason Nagata, an associate professor of pediatrics at the University of California, San Francisco who was not involved in the study, said the real-world phone-use data allowed the authors to examine how the settlement's proposed protections might come into play.

Sources indicate that the daily time limit, for example, would affect just 7.7 percent of teens in the ABCD study. If implemented by TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat, that would rise to 63 percent.

Blocking Meta access from midnight to 6 a.m. would affect more than three-quarters of Meta owners. By contrast, the same rule applied to TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat would cover nearly 90 percent of users in the ABCD study.

Recent findings suggest that there's a similar disparity for overnight notifications. Shutting them off between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m. would affect almost two-thirds of Meta owners, but 86 percent across TikTok, YouTube, Snapchat, Facebook, and Instagram.

"The study shows how difficult it can be to address social media use one manufacturer at a time when teens regularly move across many different platforms," noted Nagata, who previously used ABCD data to study smartphone use at night and during school.

Recent findings suggest that nagata noted that the JAMA study doesn't indicate how well the proposed restrictions will work in the real world. Teens might change their behavior, shift to other platforms, or develop workarounds. Nagata believes future research must evaluate which restrictions reduce problematic use and improve teen health and well-being.

The authors acknowledged other key limitations in their research. The ABCD study sample included only Android users, not iPhone users, so it may not reflect the experiences of all U.S. teens. The passive phone tracking may miss use on other devices like tablets or laptops. The study didn't capture the content viewed by users, which may affect teens differently. The estimated reach of the restrictions is also hypothetical.

Still, the researchers argue their estimates provide a baseline for understanding the restrictions' potential impact.

"The settlement will affect some teenagers but not enough,” Christakis mentioned.
