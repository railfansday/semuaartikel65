The questions Jensen Huang and Nvidia will still have to answer about Megaspeed

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Industry observers note that jensen Huang’s return as CEO to Nvidia hasn’t eased the scrutiny the company confronts. The chipmaker still faces multiple inquiries into allegations that its advanced GPUs were smuggled into China by a customer, the Singapore-based data center operator Megaspeed, in violation of US export rules designed to slow China’s development of frontier AI models.

Sources indicate that the central question for investigators is what Nvidia knew about where those chips ended up, and whether Huang’s affair with a Megaspeed executive gave the company reason to doubt Megaspeed’s assurances. Huang’s return doesn’t settle that. US Attorney General Todd Blanche has suggested Nvidia isn’t a target of an investigation, but California’s attorney general and Congress are pressing ahead, and the Securities & Exchange Commission is reportedly investigating, too.

In a recent development, the US government opened an investigation into Megaspeed in 2024 and requested information from Nvidia about its billions of dollars in sales to the tech giant, concerned that some of the chips Megaspeed bought for data center projects in Malaysia were in fact being diverted to China. Singapore also began investigating similar allegations in 2025. Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.

Recent findings suggest that diversion of this kind would be a fairly routine concern for a company like Nvidia, whose in-demand chips are the targets of smugglers and re-sellers. When it comes to compliance and liability for export control violations, Nvidia and other chip sellers need assurances that the end users of their chips are obeying the rules. However, they also cannot ignore other information that might call the reliability of those assurances into question, export control attorneys told TechCrunch.

Industry observers note that and that’s why Jensen Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was such a problem for Nvidia, likely even more so than her alleged role as a source for Chinese intelligence agencies. If Nvidia personnel, from lower-level employees up to the CEO, had reason to believe that statements from Megaspeed weren’t reliable, the attorneys remarked, that would increase the company’s legal risk.

“Government investigators likely would want to know, notwithstanding whatever formal assurances were exchanged between the parties, what anyone at Nvidia actually knew about the end use and end owners of the chips,” Dan Fisher-Owens, an attorney at the firm Berliner Corcoran & Rowe, told TechCrunch last week.

The concern Nvidia’s board faced when it decided to oust Huang is that he knew more about what Megaspeed was doing with Nvidia’s products than was reflected in any of the compliance statements. The Wall Street Journal reported that Alice Huang and her husband, Kang Wei, were part of a network of businesses that funneled chips to a manufacturer in Shanghai. Kang also reportedly introduced his wife to Jensen Huang, whom he had known for a decade.

In a recent development, on Monday, the US government added Megaspeed and its affiliates to a list of companies restricted from US trade due to national security concerns. The US also issued newly introduced rules last week barring large data centers that purchase US chips from serving customers in China and other nations seen as problematic by the US government. Nvidia, meanwhile, said it would stop selling licensed chips to China, and laid out plans to hire its first compliance officer and create a new board committee focused on export controls.

Nvidia’s board said it reinstated Huang on Monday after receiving “input from senior levels of the United States government” that left it confident his return posed no national security risk. President Donald Trump later said he called into the meeting to give them that assurance.

In a news conference Tuesday, Blanche noted that Nvidia was “a good corporate citizen” and suggested it is not the target of an investigation, but Blanche declined to answer questions about the government’s investigation into Megaspeed, which the Justice Department said was ongoing as recently as last week.

“When you say the export investigation is still ongoing, I don’t know what you’re talking about,” Blanche said.

Other authorities may be more demanding. Nvidia faces investigations by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and, reportedly, the SEC, both tied to its corporate disclosures. While many details of the investigation still aren’t known, short sellers complained about the tech giant’s decision to stop breaking out its exports to Singapore in financial filings after journalists raised concerns about chip diversion.

Members of Congress from both parties say they want answers from Nvidia, with many citing alarm about China and some questioning the White House’s role in Huang’s return. Three committees in the House of Representatives will privately interview Huang and Nvidia’s top lawyer, Tim Teter, next week. Teter reportedly intervened in the Department of Commerce’s investigation of Megaspeed last year.

“We remain committed to getting answers on the mass smuggling of Nvidia products, their diversion for Chinese military purposes, and their key role in advancing our foremost adversary’s AI capabilities,” the Republican chairs of House committees focused on foreign affairs, intelligence, and China said in a joint statement.

Industry observers note that rep. Brian Mast, the chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, has also demanded that the Department of Commerce share extensive data regarding its investigation into chip smuggling, including by Megaspeed and its affiliates.

In a recent development, the Senate Banking Committee will also question Huang in a public hearing on Dec. 3.

According to recent reports, “Mr. Huang, Nvidia executives, and the firm’s board still owe Congress answers about the company’s compliance with U.S. export controls,” Senator Elizabeth Warren, the ranking Democratic member on the committee, said in a statement. “The Trump Administration also needs to explain to Congress what ‘input’ was given and by which senior officials in the United States government to support his reinstatement.”.
