Tech Report: What causes a sudden drop in phone battery percentage?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

An aging battery or a recent software enhancement could be the reasons behind your phone's sudden battery drops.

Recent findings suggest that few things are more frustrating than noticing that your phone's battery is draining faster than usual. It gets to a point where it can no longer last a full day away from the charger. Under normal conditions, battery drain depends on what you're doing on your phone. Lighter uses like browsing the web and listening to music use less power, while taking lots of photos and playing games tend to drain your battery faster. But when your phone's battery suddenly drops without heavy use, something might be wrong.

According to recent reports, at the same time, that doesn't mean your phone's battery is dying. Software can also cause your battery to drain faster, like when you install an iOS or Android patch, or when an app is constantly running in the background. If you've been noticing that your phone's battery is draining faster, here's what you can try to fix it.

Sources indicate that your phone doesn't know exactly how much charge is left in the battery. In fact, it estimates that number based on the battery's voltage and current, and sometimes that estimate is far off from reality. Eventually, the software recalculates it, and that's when you see the battery percentage suddenly drop by a significant amount instead of gradually. This happens more often with batteries in poor condition. As lithium-ion batteries age chemically, they no longer retain their full charge capacity and may also fail when the phone needs a power boost to perform a demanding task, like opening the camera or using GPS. As a result, your phone may think the battery is much lower than it actually is.

It has been revealed that smartphones have mechanisms to prevent this from happening. On iPhones, Apple has implemented a system that detects when the battery fails to reduce performance in order to avoid these spikes in power consumption. Temperature also has a major impact, as most phones are rated to operate at temperatures between 0° and 35° C (32° to 95° F). Using your phone at temperatures above that range can significantly reduce battery life. When your phone gets too hot, the battery will most likely drain much faster. It's best to stop using it until it returns to a normal temperature. Don't put it in a refrigerator, though, as the introduction of cold air can create condensation within the warmer phone.

Sources indicate that also, it's perfectly normal to see your phone's battery drain a little faster for a few hours or even days after an update. Both iOS and Android run a series of optimizations to put all the files in their proper places, and this increases battery consumption. The good news is that this is temporary, and everything goes back to normal once the indexing process is complete. A buggy app or a process constantly running in the background, especially if it uses your location, will also drain your phone's battery promptly.

Start by closing apps running in the background to see if your battery usage returns to normal. It's also worth taking a look at your privacy settings and limiting access to your location to only when you have an app open. This prevents your phone from constantly triggering the GPS. That alone should have a significant impact on reducing battery consumption. Also, check your phone's battery settings to see exactly which apps are draining your battery the most — both iOS and Android provide details on this.

Pixel 6 and later models have a Battery Diagnostics tool under Settings. On a Samsung Galaxy, open Settings, then Battery and tap View Details. For iPhone owners, it's as simple as going to Settings and opening the Battery menu. These menus usually include details about your phone's battery health. Apple, for example, says that the latest iPhones are designed to retain 80 percent of their original battery capacity through up to 1,000 charge cycles. If your phone's battery health is below 80 percent, you should probably replace it with a upcoming one — which, at least, is much cheaper than buying a new phone.
