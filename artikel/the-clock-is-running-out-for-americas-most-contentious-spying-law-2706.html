Weeks before the end of the legislative session, some members of Congress are scrambling to reauthorize a controversial warrantless wiretapping authority, possibly by attaching it to a must-pass military spending bill. Sen. Mike Rounds (R-SD) told Politicothat Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act (FISA) could be attached to the National Defense Authorization Act. It’s the latest in a grueling fight over a major spying rule that hasn’t technically lapsed.

“We don’t have two weeks to work just on FISA,” said Rounds, a member of the Senate Select Committee on Intelligence. “But if it’s amended onto the NDAA then you can move it forward and get that done in time.”.

It has been revealed that FISA allows for the warrantless surveillance of certain foreigners’ communications, ostensibly on national security grounds. In part because of FISA’s broad scope, Congress revisits it every few years, both to reauthorize the authority and to reform it. The last effort to do both failed. Despite pressure from President Donald Trump, Congress failed to reauthorize FISA over the summer, in part because leadership wouldn’t allow any amendments — particularly attempts to limit its sweeping scope — to be debated or introduced. (Trump didn’t help matters when he made Bill Pulte the acting director of national intelligence. Pulte, who reportedly bragged about purging one-third of the DNI staff, is now the director of federal housing.) But the surveillance authority didn’t actually lapse because the FISA court rectified surveillance under Section 702 back in March, meaning the surveillance state can keep chugging along as is for another four months.

According to recent reports, so why the urgency? If you haven’t heard, there’s about to be a new Congress in town — one that is even less likely to give Trump seemingly unlimited spying powers without some semblance of reform.

Support for — and opposition to — FISA isn’t a clear-cut partisan issue. Last time around, a bipartisan coalition led by House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) and Rep. Jim Himes (D-CT), the ranking member of the House Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence, led the campaign for a “clean” reauthorization of FISA. Their efforts were defeated largely because of holdouts from the Freedom Caucus, whose privacy concerns echoed those of liberal lawmakers. FISA’s critics wanted reform, not abolition. Their goal was to make it harder for intelligence agencies to use the statute, which is intended for foreign targets, to access Americans’ communications.

“It was remarkable watching two dozen House Republicans stand up to the establishment, stand up to Mike Johnson, and fight to reform FISA,” said Michael Baick, an organizer with the OpenAI boycott campaign QuitGPT, which has been advocating for FISA reform to stop AI surveillance. “This has always been a bipartisan problem where Americans of all stripes can agree, and trying to make deals with the establishment to maintain the status quo, while the ground is shifting, is just short-sighted and missing what this moment really is.”.

“I think it’s a Hail Mary by surveillance hawks to try to ram through what they wanted to do the entire time.”.

It has been revealed that johnson and Himes were adamantly opposed to any reining in of Section 702, stressing the need for a so-called “clean reauthorization.” Both claimed there had already been reforms. “It ain’t the same FISA,” Rep. Jim Jordan (R-OH) declared in April.

But the changes reformers most wanted were missing. Among them were a warrant requirement and an end to what critics say are major loopholes. Under Section 702, intelligence agencies can pull the communications of non-US persons not in the United States — that is, foreign nationals outside the country. But federal agencies like the FBI and NSA can buy Americans’ data from private brokers, a practice FBI director Kash Patel admits is ongoing. And intelligence agencies can use Section 702 to obtain the communications of non-US persons who are communicating with Americans — a workaround known as the “backdoor search loophole.”.

“I think it’s a Hail Mary by surveillance hawks to try to ram through what they wanted to do the entire time,” Don Bell, senior policy counsel at the Constitution Project of the Project on Government Oversight, told The Verge. “This is something everyone knew was coming down the pike. There have been months to do this, and I don’t think anyone is saying don’t reauthorize at all. The argument is this is an incredibly powerful surveillance tool, and there needs to be guardrails in place to protect people’s constitutional rights.”.

Sources indicate that himes, arguably the Democratic Party’s biggest champion of FISA, seems willing to give Trump one more win before the blue wave crashes down in Washington. “I made it clear to some very senior administration officials that they should get this thing done in the lame-duck,” he told The Hill, “because it’s, you know, going to be a very different 702 that gets reauthorized in the first quarter of next year.”.

But Himes didn’t have the votes this summer, and it’s even less likely that he’ll have the votes for a clean reauthorization now.
