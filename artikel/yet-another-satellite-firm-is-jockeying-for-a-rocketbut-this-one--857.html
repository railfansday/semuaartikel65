Yet another satellite firm is jockeying for a rocket—but this one makes sense

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Finnish satellite developer ICEYE announced Tuesday that it is seeking to buy Italian rocket-maker Avio for more than $2.2 billion in a transaction aimed at creating Europe’s first true vertically integrated end-to-end space tech giant.

It has been revealed that the announcement came as an all-cash voluntary tender offer from an Italian subsidiary of ICEYE to Avio’s shareholders, led by the Italian defense conglomerate Leonardo. Avio remarked its board of directors “has taken note of the notice” from ICEYE and is considering the purchase offer.

In a recent development, avio builds the Vega C rocket, a solid-fueled launcher designed to carry up to 3.3 metric tons (7,300 pounds) of payload to low-Earth orbit. The Vega C has flown successfully seven times in eight attempts since 2022. It is a follow-on to an earlier version of the Vega rocket that amassed a 20-for-22 record since debuting in 2012.

ICEYE is, by far, the most successful space startup in Europe, where access to private capital, regulatory barriers, and fragmented markets often hinder startup growth, according to a recent report from the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development. ICEYE’s valuation stands at more than $12 billion after the company’s most recent fundraising round in June.

In a statement, ICEYE said it wants to join forces with Avio to meet Europe’s need for “sovereign space and defense capabilities.” ICEYE manufactures and operates its own satellites for radar surveillance and sells data to commercial customers and national governments, including Ukraine, which uses ICEYE imagery for targeting and tracking Russian troop movements.

“Governments need control of their critical space infrastructure, and Europe needs it most,” said Rafal Modrzewski, CEO and co-founder of ICEYE. “Avio has built the launch and propulsion capability Europe relies on. With ICEYE’s long-term capital behind it, we can integrate the supply chain, grow output and strengthen the European programs that already depend on it. Together we can build, launch and operate sovereign space systems end-to-end, made in Europe for governments across the world.”.

Avio oversees the consortium of European companies that produces the Vega C rocket and conducts final assembly of Vega C rocket stages at its factory near Rome. For most of the Vega program’s history, Avio handed the rockets to the French launch provider Arianespace, which managed marketing, sales, and launch day operations in French Guiana. Avio recently took over those responsibilities to bring the entire Vega C operation in-house.

It has been revealed that ICEYE has built and launched 84 radar reconnaissance satellites, providing routine overhead imagery of locations around the world, even during darkness or cloud cover. In addition to its commercial satellite fleet, ICEYE has sold turnkey satellites directly to national governments, including Finland, Poland, Germany, Sweden, Portugal, Greece, and the Netherlands.

Last month, ICEYE announced a partnership with Nokia, another Finnish company, to develop a low-Earth orbit satellite constellation to support European connectivity needs, particularly in the defense and security sectors. Ukraine’s Ministry of Defense announced a preliminary agreement, saying it intends to work with ICEYE and Nokia on the constellation, days after Ukraine reported new restrictions on its access to Starlink and Starshield broadband services offered by SpaceX.

“Modern warfare has proven that reliable communications are a matter of national security,” Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said. “We must all rely on capabilities that we can control.”.

There’s a long way to go before ICEYE and Nokia can start deploying their constellation, but their goal is to begin launching 50 satellites per year starting in 2028. The companies have not disclosed expected figures for throughput and latency on the network, or a total number of satellites for global or regional coverage. Depending on altitude, it would likely require hundreds or thousands of satellites. SpaceX has more than 11,000 operating satellites in its Starlink constellation today.

In a recent development, what is clear, however, is that ICEYE’s future radar and broadband satellites will need rides to space, and Avio’s Vega C rocket needs business. The primary customer for Vega C is the European Space Agency, and the rest of the rocket’s future schedule is largely filled with satellites from European national governments. Vega C’s purely commercial business line is thin.

Through the proposed transaction, ICEYE says it would secure more access to launch services, and Avio would receive investment for higher production and more frequent flights of the Vega rocket family, which averages fewer than three per year.

The most recent data indicates a dedicated Vega C launch sells for approximately $55 million, just shy of the commercial price for a much more powerful dedicated SpaceX Falcon 9. Nearly all of ICEYE’s satellites, except for the first view demo spacecraft, have released on Falcon 9 rideshare missions, which SpaceX intends to phase out after 2028. SpaceX sold rideshare launch slots for satellites of ICEYE’s size and mass for about $700,000. After accounting for potential savings after ICEYE’s potential purchase of Avio, an ICEYE satellite might be able to launch on Vega C for two to three times that price.

Sources indicate that avio and the European Space Agency are jointly funding the development of a fresh version of Vega, the Vega E, which replaces the top part of Vega C with a new higher-performing methane-fueled upper stage. ESA and Avio also signed a nearly $50 million contract last year to begin designing a reusable upper stage similar in concept and appearance to SpaceX’s Starship, although much smaller. The idea is to complete early design work on the reusable upper stage as a potential replacement for Vega E.

In a recent development, business results seem to bear out the benefits of combining launch and satellite services under one roof. SpaceX’s Starlink network and Falcon 9 rocket would not have been as successful without the other. Rocket Lab, the No. 2 US launch firm by launch rate, rapidly branched out into satellite manufacturing after establishing the reliability of its Electron rocket. Pretty much every US launch provider has a role in satellite production or in-space services, or aims to, except for United Launch Alliance.

Sources indicate that the coming launch crunch precipitated by SpaceX’s plans to retire the Falcon 9 has underscored the importance of assured access to space. SpaceX is prioritizing Starship launches for its own Starlink network and future Starmind AI satellites.

One of SpaceX’s competitors in the satellite broadband business, AST Space Mobile, revealed last week that it is developing its own rocket to launch its communications network. It has introduced most of its satellites to date on Falcon 9. Another company, Cowboy Space, intends to put orbiting data centers into space, bringing it into competition with SpaceX’s Starmind initiative. Cowboy Space, too, recently announced it was working on its own rocket.

Recent findings suggest that there are reasons to disbelieve both companies’ launcher intends. Neither has any rocket development experience, and both are targeting first flights in 2028, a bewilderingly fast path to the launch pad. There are reasons to be skeptical of ICEYE and Avio, too, but if they do join forces, the combined tech giant would boast established satellite and rocket production lines and backing from European governments seeking to reclaim sovereignty amid fraying ties with the United States.
