Here’s what a strong password looks like and how to check whether yours would hold up against hackers, plus a few habits that keep accounts safer.

It has been revealed that remember the old days when you'd try to use "1234" or "password" as your password? There really was never a time when passwords like those were secure. Thankfully, nowadays, most services don't even permit you to use passwords that aren't secure enough. They require a combination of letters, numbers and symbols along with different letter cases before accepting your chosen password. It makes passwords tougher to remember on the one hand, but it's essential in this day and age to ensure that your password can't be easily cracked by nefarious individuals or even AI systems.

But even if you think you have created a password that Robert Langdon from The Da Vinci Code himself couldn't crack, how do you truly know how secure it is? Usually, if it includes all the elements noted above, especially if the letters do not connect to spell an actual word, you're in a good spot.

Recent findings suggest that to know for sure, you can run it through a free password strength checker, such as the one at Security.org. However, remember that creating a secure password is just one of the steps you can take to be safer online.

It's vital to understand what makes a good password in the first place. It should be at least 12 characters, and in fact, many sites do not let you submit a password that is less than that anyway. Along with the aforementioned upper and lower case letters, numbers and symbols, keep in mind that the letters should not spell a discernible, and certainly not an identifiable word like your surname. They should also not be consecutive in the alphabet, like "LMNOP," or in a row on a keyboard, like "QWERTY." The same goes for numbers, which should be random instead of something like your birthday or street address. The letters and numbers should not be repeated as well, like "BBB" or "555.".

Once you create a password you think is secure, enter it into the Security.org checker, which evaluates it and provides feedback. As you type, the site demonstrates how long it would take a computer to crack the password. The more characters you add, the longer this duration becomes. I went from a password with letters and numbers that would take four years to figure out to one with symbols that bumps it to 2 million years before someone could crack it. It's safe to say both are secure, but the latter is more secure. When I tried using a simple word with consecutive numbers, it advised that the password could be cracked in three days.

It has been revealed that when creating an account, you might see an option to "create a strong password" automatically after which you can save it to password memory, like Apple MacBook's Passwords app or a third-party password manager like 1Password. It's a good idea to use these even over signing in with an existing Google, Apple or other account. There are convenience benefits to doing that, but for extra security, create a upcoming login.

There are pros and cons of using a password manager over an authenticator app, but there's no reason you can't use both. If the site offers passkeys versus passwords, these are worth using. They can be more secure, relying on a private key accessed through biometrics (your fingerprint or face) or a PIN.

In a recent development, along with having a secure password, it is also important to make every password unique. If you use the same password for everything, no matter how secure, what happens if someone gains access to it from one account? This could happen through a breach or even human error, and it would unlock all your accounts and services. Keeping passwords secure and unique to every service and changing them every 60 to 90 days or immediately if a breach has been reported will keep you as safe as possible.
