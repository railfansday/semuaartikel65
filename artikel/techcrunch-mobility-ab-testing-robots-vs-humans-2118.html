Waymo pulling its robotaxis off freeways (again) and Zoox robotaxis launching on the Uber app illustrate the push and pull between progress and setbacks in the autonomous vehicle industry.

Industry observers note that there are signs of growth. For instance, Tesla has now surpassed Waymo in Texas in the number of robotaxis registered with the state (Tesla has 1,507 and Waymo has 1,345). But does that translate to market dominance?

A better metric, if we’re trying to gauge who is leading, would be commercial driverless miles. And if we’re measuring cost-efficiency, another useful metric would be driverless miles per vehicle. By those measures, Waymo is still ahead, based on data available to me. But with Waymo’s recent freeways setback and Tesla’s push to expand its robotaxi fleet with Cybercabs, that lead could shrink.

Not that there needs to be a winner, by the way. Transportation is deeply fragmented, and there is room for multiple players in the U.S., not to mention the rest of the world.

Recent findings suggest that A recent discussion with my Autonocast co-host Ed Niedermeyer, whose book, “Elon Take the Wheel,” comes out December 1, got me thinking about how Las Vegas could soon become a real world A/B test of sorts for human-driven vs. autonomous ride-hailing services.

Recent findings suggest that uber, as mentioned above, just added Zoox to its ride-hailing app in Las Vegas. For now, riders don’t have much of a choice. Uber customers can increase their chances of getting a Zoox robotaxi, and they can always decline a robot ride. But they can’t directly select a robotaxi. Instead, Uber riders will be matched with one if it’s available.

According to recent reports, uber mentioned that will soon change. The company plans to introduce a dedicated autonomous option in its app as “availability and reliability improve,” Uber CEO Dara Khosrowshahi said during the company’s third-quarter earnings call in November.

Las Vegas will also become the first U.S. city where riders can access two fully driverless partners through the Uber app, he noted. (Hyundai’s self-driving subsidiary Motional is also an Uber partner.).

According to recent reports, that would give Uber riders a meaningful choice: a human driver or a robotaxi, and potentially even which autonomous service to use. And it could offer a revealing look at what customers actually prefer when given that choice. Price could tip the balance, too.

It makes me wonder whether Las Vegas will become the next battleground for organized labor as robotaxis begin competing directly with human drivers.

It has been revealed that you might have seen the Wall Street Journal scoop that General Motors is working with an executive search firm to identify possible candidates to replace CEO Mary Barra. Rumors of her imminent departure have swirled for months, but this time there appears to be real work happening behind the scenes.

In a recent development, I reached out to a few little birds to learn more and here’s what they’re telling me. And, for the record, GM declined to comment.

GM has hired executive search firm Heidrick & Struggles, according to one source, who noted it’s possible that the board has other executive search firms working on this as well. The board is taking a two-track approach to its search, which is occurring simultaneously.

The first is focused on traditional yet transformative automotive CEOs. One name that has come up from the executive search firm is José Muñoz, who is president and CEO of Hyundai. The board is also interested in someone less traditional. The firm has apparently floated names like Doug Field, who recently became CEO of Mind Robotics, and Larry Culp of GE, according to a source with direct information.

There are internal GM folks who are also in the mix, including Sterling Anderson, co-founder of self-driving trucks company Aurora who became chief product officer at GM in May 2025, and Paul Jacobson, the company’s executive vice president and chief financial officer.
