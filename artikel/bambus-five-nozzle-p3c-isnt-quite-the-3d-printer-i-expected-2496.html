Bambu’s five-nozzle P3C isn’t quite the 3D printer I expected

Three weeks ago, Bambu Lab introduced the state of the art in home printing tech at $2,499 — now, you can get many of its fancy latest features for less than half the price.

Like that bigger H3C, the newly introduced $1,149 Bambu P3C can also swap between materials on the fly, mix different colors to produce much of the rainbow, mix soft and hard materials, even mix different sizes of nozzle and different diameters of filament so you can switch between high speed and fine detail in a single print.

But Bambu cut more corners than I expected to hit the lower $1,149 / £999 / €1,149 launch price today, and I think they made more of a tinkerer’s 3D printer than one suited for either beginners or pros.

Recent findings suggest that some of the changes were completely expected, like its smaller 256 x 256 x 260mm print area (much like Bambu’s other P-series printers), and that it supports seven automatically swapping tools instead of nine. (Seven is still quite a lot when affordable toolchangers like the $849 Snapmaker U1 typically offer just four.).

But unlike the H3C, the P3C doesn’t come fully loaded, and it doesn’t load itself. While it has slots for seven rolls of filament and up to seven independent tools (including thicker-than-usual 2.85mm filament, or pens, cutting blades, and debossing and foil transfer tools if you want it to double as a Cricut), it only comes with five 0.4mm print nozzles by default.

And while five is still one more color than you’d get with an $800 Bambu P2S Combo, the 3D printer I’d buy for myself, the Bambu P3C’s automated material system (AMS) is the first Bambu has shipped without any motors to automatically load your filament.

Don’t get me wrong: You can still keep five colors of filament ready to go at a moment’s notice, one more than the P2S, by keeping them loaded into the P3C’s five independent nozzles. You can also buy and fairly easily install two more printheads as well, though they cost up to $100 each.

But loading those nozzles is a slow and surprisingly manual process where you have to shove a string of filament down a tube, quite a long distance by hand, after waiting for the machine to preheat each one, and it feels like a step backwards after years using the earlier motorized versions of Bambu’s AMS. Then again, the P3C doesn’t have to spend time and filament purging between colors like the P2S does.

Industry observers note that I expect many 3D printing enthusiasts will see $1,149 for the Bambu P3C as a pretty good deal, assuming they haven’t written off the company’s gadgets after Bambu’s open source snafus. Even five nozzles is enough to load all five rolls of Bambu’s “PLA Palette” cyan, magenta, yellow, black, and white (CMYKW) filaments for color mixing to produce beautiful creations like the company’s fresh hummingbird test print, which came out almost flawlessly on my first try.

But you should know that color mixing isn’t necessarily a foolproof way to produce some colors, like the brown tree branch that’s supposed to be the hummingbird’s perch, and I’m not looking forward to manually pulling and pushing a roll of brown filament to change that. Bambu indicates I should probably try a feature called “mixed color sublayer” for better results, and I also suppose I could daisy-chain a normal motorized AMS to one of the P3C’s printheads, as that is a supported workflow. But that’s why I’m suggesting we’re in tinkerer territory.

I asked Bambu about the decision to not include filament-loading motors in the P3C’s AMS system, and here’s part of the statement I got back from spokesperson Karlie Hou: “The AMS 3P’s design deliberately omits the auto‑loading feature as part of the effort to offer premium multi‑head performance at roughly half the price of the flagship H3C system, allowing a far broader audience — hobbyists, small studios, and educational institutions — to enjoy premium multi‑head, multi‑color printing experience.”.

Industry observers note that the company claims it should take around five minutes to manually reload five nozzles. “In the next [software] upgrade, we aim to shrink the total hands‑on time for loading five spools to under one minute,” the company writes, though that seems like a tall order.

Recent findings suggest that maybe I’ll put the P3C to a year-long test like I did with the P2S, and see how often I actually need something CMYKW can’t do for me, and whether the filament stays dry when left in the printheads for a long time. The question is: What printers should I test against? Are there any upcoming ones I should wait for?
