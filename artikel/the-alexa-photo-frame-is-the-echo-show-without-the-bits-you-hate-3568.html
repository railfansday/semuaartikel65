It has been revealed that I have to face facts: I’m a digital hoarder. My son graduated high school this year, and to mark the occasion, I made him a scrapbook of his 12 years of school. He loved it, but one reaction surprised me: He noted he’d never seen most of these photos. Unlike my childhood — documented in photo albums sitting on shelves for anyone to browse — the photographic evidence of my son’s childhood is sitting on my phone. Cue the mom-guilt.

While a few photos are framed around our house, my phone holds thousands of pictures of my son that he’s never seen. And that just feels wrong. The solution to this — getting photos off your phone and into your home — is the pitch behind Amazon’s newest gadget, the Alexa Photo Frame, which starts at $199.99.

It has been revealed that A smart digital photo frame that uses AI to surface your best photos automatically, the Frame is also an Alexa smart speaker. It seems Amazon has finally made the product its owners have been asking for: a smart speaker that shows pictures — nothing more, nothing less. No rotating screen serving up a random smorgasbord of Alexa suggestions, weather, and news. No camera. And, best of all, no display ads. The only real question is, what took so long?

The only real question is, what took so long?

For years, Amazon’s Echo Show smart displays did a nice job displaying photos, but I couldn’t ever have them display only photos; there was always some rotating content showing Alexa suggestions or other cruft. In 2023, the company rolled out a more expensive dedicated photos edition of the Echo Show 8, but rapidly discontinued it. Then, last year, Echo Indicates started regularly showing full-screen display ads for motor oil and recliners between my family photos — and I quickly banished them to little-seen corners of my home.

The Alexa Photo Frame feels a bit like Amazon’s apology to customers who just wanted a goddamn digital photo frame — albeit an expensive one. The Frame does exactly what I wanted the Indicates to do with photos, without showing anything else.

One word of caution: while it only shows photos today, the Frame’s 10-inch touchscreen is functionally the same as the Echo Shows, meaning nothing is stopping Amazon from adding ads down the line — which is what happened with the Shows. For now, Amazon has remarked the device only shows photos and does not display ads. In my three weeks of testing, that’s all it’s shown, and it’s done a nice job thanks to its AI-powered photo curation feature.

As a dedicated photo device, the Alexa Photo Frame is great. Photos look lovely on its anti-glare 1280 x 800, 16:10 aspect ratio screen, and the AI-powered photo selection does the hard work of curating your photos.

According to recent reports, my favorite feature is the Daily Memories collection, the Frame’s default album, which pulls up pictures from this time in past years. All week I’ve been seeing past Thanksgiving celebrations and shots of my now-teenage kids sitting on Santa’s lap as toddlers. It’s been genuinely delightful.

The catch is you have to use Amazon Photos. Unlike other smart frames, it has no option to import directly from Google Photos or Apple’s iCloud, where your photos most likely live. If you pay for Amazon Prime, though, Amazon Photos offers free unlimited storage, and I could import my iPhone’s photo library just by opening the app and turning on automatic backup. It took a little over a day to back up my 100,000-plus photo library.

When the Frame shows vertical images, instead of cropping them or adding black bars as some frames do, it uses AI to pair them with similar pictures: my daughter Rollerblading alongside my son playing tennis; my daughter on the beach next to a shot of her playing in the snow; my son cuddling our dog next to one of him snuggling with our cat. It does a good job of pairing similar images, and I especially like when it serves up then-and-now pictures; it’s fun to see baby pictures next to shots of my grown-up kids.

Sources indicate that amazon claims Alexa uses a combination of faces, time, and context to sort photos for you. It looks for smiles and good lighting while skipping screenshots and receipts, and also that close-up of the mole on your shoulder. Daily Memories is my favorite, but you can choose from auto-updating collections of specific people (not pets, sadly), create albums with specific images, and have multiple collections rotating at once. You can also invite family and friends to add their photos to the Frame.

According to recent reports, the Frame is an Alexa smart speaker, and the on-board voice assistant can answer questions about your photos. When a picture of my kids in face masks visiting Santa popped up, I asked Alexa what year it was, and it answered correctly: “2021.” Then I asked to see more photos from that day, and it pulled up all the images from our visit and a slideshow play button — this would be a neat way to show off your holiday snaps when friends visit. You can also ask it for more specific requests: “Show me all the photos of my son playing tennis” or “Show me all the photos of my daughter playing bass.”.

Digital photo frames I’ve used from Aura and Skylight rely more heavily on you fiddling with an app to add latest pictures. Amazon’s special sauce is that once set up, it’s largely hands-free. Of course, Google has done this with its Nest Hub smart displays for years, and they have long been my favorite digital photo frames. But Nest Hub’s hardware is aging, and its algorithms aren’t as good (I have definitely seen some mole shots). The Alexa Photo Frame’s semi-matte screen also displays photos better with less glare. However, the Frame’s screen isn’t as good as the $229 Aura Aspen, with its higher 1600 x 1200 resolution and clever 4:3 aspect ratio that displays both horizontal and portrait images beautifully.

It has been revealed that along with the Frame’s white mat and actual frame (in black or wood), it looks more like a photo frame and less like a piece of tech than an Echo Show or Nest Hub. Don’t get me wrong: The Frame is unmistakably a screen, but like all digital photo frames, its benefit over a regular photo frame is fresh images every few seconds, minutes, hours, or once a day.

Sources indicate that I tested the walnut model, which costs an extra $30; there’s also a birch model and the cheaper black option. It’s very solid and well-made. A light sensor adjusts the display based on ambient light (with the option to shut it off completely in a dark room). It has four microphones and a pair of stereo speakers for Alexa, plus a physical mute button to turn the voice assistant off and volume buttons. Unlike Aura or Skylight frames, it doesn’t play video or Live Photos, only static images, but it has a unique feature that lets you add a voice note to a photo that plays when you tap on it. I didn’t find much use for this personally, but Amazon bills this as a gifting feature, since it can play a message when the recipient sets it up. Probably a good option for the grandparents.

Industry observers note that the other feature the Frame has over the competition is that it is a full-fledged Alexa Plus speaker and can do everything an Echo Dot can, including controlling your smart home, setting timers and alarms, and booking you a table at a local restaurant. Unlike an Echo Show, it doesn’t use its display to show information, just photos. The Frame’s built-in speaker is fine for podcasts and radio — a step up from your iPhone speakers, but not a significant one. For music, pairing it with an Echo or a Bluetooth speaker in the Alexa app is the way to go.

If it could display a clock over the photos, like Apple’s HomeView smart display, it would make a great bedside table device, because there’s no camera and the screen goes totally black at night, so it won’t disturb you. But it really belongs in a living room where everyone can see all the photos that have been stuck on your phone for years.

Compared to the competition, Alexa adds real value here. As much as I love the Aura Aspen that I reviewed last year, my husband regularly complained that he was bored of the photos because I kept forgetting to add new ones to the album. My son came home from college this weekend and mentioned the exact same thing about the Skylight on our wall. Now, this is user error, and if I were more proactive about updating those devices, it wouldn’t be an issue. But with the Frame it’s really nice not to have to think about it and still get fresh pictures.

It has been revealed that however, Aura is completely subscription-free, whereas you need Amazon Prime or a $19.99-a-month Alexa Plus sub to get the Frame’s intelligent photo curation features, voice search, and photo pairing. (It includes 90 days of Alexa Plus.) Without the sub, you can just upload images to the Frame from Amazon Photos.

Compared to the competition, Alexa adds real value.

At $200, the Frame is a bit too expensive. While comparable in price to the Aura and Skylight frames, it costs the same as the Echo Show 8, which has the same Alexa-powered photo curation and voice features, but adds a camera, a better, if smaller, screen (8.7 inches / 1340 x 800), better speakers, and a more functional touchscreen. You can also pay $2.99 a month to remove ads from the Show with Amazon’s Photo Gallery Unlimited subscription.

Recent findings suggest that while many people don’t want or need that extra hardware of the Echo Show, it somehow feels wrong that Amazon charges the same amount for less. However, the Frame is already seeing its first significant discount: It’s down to $115 for Black Friday. At that price, it’s an easy buy for households that want a smart digital photo frame that can also set a timer. If Amazon added the option of a clock, it would be almost perfect.
