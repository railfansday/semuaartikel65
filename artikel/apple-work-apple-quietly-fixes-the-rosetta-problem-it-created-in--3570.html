A very simple line in the macOS 27.2 enterprise release notes is a massive fix that will eliminate a lot of help desk tickets I’ve gotten over the last two months: “Rosetta is no longer removed when upgrading to macOS 27.” I’m glad it shipped, but it’s a fix for a problem Apple created on purpose, in a release where Rosetta was/is supposed to keep working, a full year before the deadline for removing support actually comes.

Recent findings suggest that apple’s own documentation claims Rosetta “will continue as a general-purpose compatibility tool through macOS 27.” That’s the sentence that IT teams planned around with Golden Gate. Your Intel apps work through 27, and you have until macOS 28 next fall to sort out the stragglers that still haven’t shipped an Apple Silicon build. I’ve got a handful of vendors that I am working on as of this writing. One of them is likely getting replaced.

It has been revealed that the same documentation also said: “If Rosetta was previously installed, it will not be automatically restored upon upgrading to macOS 27.” So Rosetta was still available and supported, but it just wasn’t there anymore after an upgrade. If they launch an Intel app, the user gets a dialog, reinstalls it, and moves on. The problem is that for various enterprise tools/agents, the app just refused to launch. This creates a bunch of tickets that IT has to resolve. Apple clearly didn’t test this removal well enough.

Industry observers note that I keep coming back to what this was supposed to accomplish. Rosetta still worked in macOS 27. You could reinstall it in about thirty seconds. Apple wasn’t blocking anything, but it was just making you do it again. There was literally no reason to do this. It was essentially a warning shot.

It was a way to make sure everyone noticed Rosetta’s days are coming to an end. IT already knew this, though. Removing it and breaking the app is the worst available option because the user finds out first and the help desk second.

Recent findings suggest that when macOS 28 arrives next fall and reduces Rosetta to a subset aimed at older games and utilities, that’s fine. It’s been telegraphed for two years, Apple has been clear about the timeline, and any team that hasn’t dealt with its Intel apps by then had plenty of notice. Deprecations are supposed to be on a schedule.

This wasn’t that; it was a behavior change within a release that the documentation mentioned would be safe, and it took until 27.2 for Apple to walk it back.

Worth noting from the same release: three of the enterprise changes in 27.2 are around Platform SSO. The new login experience is now used by default only with OpenID or when Touch ID is required, Platform SSO can send additional HTTP headers to your identity provider, and password sync was fixed for devices that registered under macOS 26 or earlier.

Apple is allowed to deprecate Rosetta, and they probably kept it longer than I would have guessed. They are allowed to put a date on it, and they did. They supported it probably two years longer than I thought they should. What it shouldn’t do is break the thing a year early in a release where its own documentation promised the thing would work. IT teams spend a lot of effort translating Apple’s roadmap into something they can use for their organization goals, and that only works if the roadmap is predictable. If they were going to allow you to reinstall something, there was no reason to remove it in the first place.
