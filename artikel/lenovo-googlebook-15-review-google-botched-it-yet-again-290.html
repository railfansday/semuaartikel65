Lenovo Googlebook 15 review: Google botched it, yet again

In a recent development, the Lenovo Googlebook 15 is a classic case of a product that shouldn't exist. Not because of the hardware, which is as premium as its $1,099 price tag would indicate, but because of the software. Lenovo paired a 15.3-inch 120Hz OLED display with an array of ports and an understated silk-touch finish, and put it inside a 2.84-pound featherweight body. Google then proved it doesn’t know Android that well by thinking it would suit a desktop OS. The result is a product no one should buy. Not just this one, but all Googlebooks. They’re that useless. Lenovo gets top marks for the hardware, but Google gets none for the software.

It has been revealed that I don’t know why the Lenovo Googlebook 15 exists. More importantly, I don’t think Google can defend why it does, either. Not just this one… but all Googlebooks.

It has been revealed that make no mistake: this isn't on Lenovo. On paper and in the hand, the hardware here is phenomenal. The 15.3-inch 2,8K 120Hz OLED display is vibrant with solid touch response, and Lenovo also packed quad Dolby Atmos speakers, an array of ports, and a Glowbar RGB alert bar into a 2.84-pound featherweight body. This would not be out of place on any true premium laptop.

Which makes what Google did to it even harder to forgive. We’ve been down this road before. We’ve been down this road before. Google created Chromebooks as simple, $200 to $300 browser-first machines, and through sheer determination and low pricing, carved out a real niche. Those early laptops had clear limits, but they were forgivable because they were affordable. Then came Chromebook Plus in 2023, pushing prices from $399 up toward $749 on the theory that better specs and AI perks justified a bigger spend.

From that trajectory, it’s no surprise Google tried to move even further upmarket with the Googlebook. Given how half-baked many of Google’s hardware and software pivots are, it’s also no surprise that Googlebook OS is the most half-baked of them all. Why? Because running Android apps sounds great in theory until you remember that most of them still lack proper desktop features on foldables and tablets, let alone when you try to use them as your primary computer.

The crazier part is Google’s hubris. The company had enough confidence in this half-finished platform to talk five prominent PC makers (Acer, Asus, Dell, HP, and Lenovo) into launching Googlebooks. The problem is that Google paired their hardware with an operating system that makes Windows Vista look like Windows 11. Seriously: do not buy any Googlebook.

Google wants you to pay a flagship premium for a platform it hasn’t bothered to finish. It’s almost certainly destined for the Killed by Google scrapyard, and that’s where it belongs. Google has yet again proved it doesn’t understand desktop computing, and all five PC makers should rightly think twice before trusting Google with a upcoming category again. It’s time for Google to leave desktop computing to the adults in the room.

Pricing is where the Lenovo Googlebook 15 and all Googlebooks immediately trip over themselves. The cheapest Googlebook from each of the five PC makers ranges from $899 to $1,299, with the base Lenovo Googlebook 15 variant starting at $1,099.99. That gets you an Intel Core Ultra 5 325 processor, 16GB of LPDDR5X RAM, and 256GB of storage. Stepping up to the 512GB configuration pushes the price to $1,149.99 (though Lenovo throws its "AI Mouse" into the box), while European buyers face an eye-watering €1,379 sticker price.

At $500 or $600, you can forgive rough software edges as the cost of admission. At $1,100, forgiveness evaporates. That price puts the Googlebook 15 directly in the crosshairs of Apple's MacBook Air, Apple's cheaper MacBook Neo, and dozens of capable Windows 11 ultrabooks. All of those machines run mature desktop operating systems with decades of pro-grade software behind them. Crucially, Microsoft and Apple actually know how to build desktop platforms, and Google’s naivety shines right through in Googlebook OS.

Google is asking customers to pay full flagship laptop prices for the privilege of beta-testing a first-generation platform. It’s barely ready for a free beta release, let alone a four-figure price tag. That creates an obvious chicken-and-egg trap: mainstream buyers will not spend over a thousand dollars on a machine that relies on mobile apps, and without a massive install base, major developers have zero incentive to build proper desktop-class apps for Googlebook OS.

Simply put, there’s no reason anyone should root for the Googlebook, from computer makers and developers to retailers and customers.

Sources indicate that strip away Google’s half-baked OS, and Lenovo’s engineering prowess shines. Picking up the Lenovo Googlebook 15 for the first time is a pleasant surprise because a 15.3-inch laptop has no business feeling this light yet premium. Weighing 2.84 pounds and measuring 0.63 inches thick, the magnesium-aluminum and carbon-fiber chassis is lighter than my 14-inch MacBook Pro and feels much better in the hand. This is one of the few Lenovo laptops to feature a textured coating, reminiscent of Asus’ Ceraluminum, and the soft, unglazed-porcelain feel on the off-white Cloud Gray chassis is beautifully designed and made.

The 15.3-inch 2.8K (2880 x 1800) OLED touchscreen is the star of the show. Text looks razor-sharp, blacks are properly inky, and the 120Hz refresh rate keeps scrolling fluid. It is an ideal canvas for running two browser windows side by side or kicking back with HDR movies on a flight. Lenovo also nailed the port layout by placing a 40Gbps Thunderbolt 4 port on both sides of the chassis alongside a full-size HDMI 2.0 port, a 10Gbps USB-A port, and a headphone jack. Being able to charge from either side without carrying a dongle is how every modern laptop should be built.

Then there’s biometrics: some people prefer face unlock, others prefer a fingerprint sensor. The Lenovo Googlebook 15 gives you both choices, like any good Android phone, and while it might seem superfluous to some, it’s a pattern I’d like to see on all laptops.

According to recent reports, then there is the Glowbar, an exterior LED strip mounted along the lid near the hinge in a deliberate throwback to the original Chromebook Pixel. When the laptop is closed, it acts as a handy battery fuel gauge and pulses with a Gemini gradient during voice sessions. Across a table, it is a neat visual cue. Once you open the lid to actually use the computer, however, it’s as pointless as much of the OS, since you can’t see a single pixel of it.

The only real problem with the Lenovo Googlebook 15 is a terminal one: Googlebook OS.

In a recent development, built on a hybrid of Android 17 and ChromeOS windowing, it feels like the significant-manufacturer equivalent of prompting AI to build this and forgetting the details. It looks pretty, then falls apart in seconds.

Recent findings suggest that this is not new to Android. The chief complaint with Android foldables and tablets is that Android apps are poorly optimized for larger, more varied form factors. It's no surprise, then, that they are incredibly poorly optimized for desktop. The core issue is that Google still thinks scaled-up Android apps are an acceptable substitute for desktop software.

Yes, you can resize Play Store apps into floating windows, and Google touts optimized versions of apps like Adobe Photoshop, Lightroom, and Spotify. In practice, most of them still behave like stretched tablet apps. Basic desktop muscle memory breaks constantly: right-click menus are inconsistent, click-and-drag gestures don't always register where you expect, and UI elements feel oversized and awkward when you navigate with a trackpad rather than a fingertip.

In a recent development, more often than not, running the browser-based web app version of a service works better than installing its native Android counterpart. That begs an uncomfortable question: if web apps are still doing the heavy lifting, what exactly is the $1,100 upgrade over a $450 Chromebook Plus?

Under the hood, running ARM-compiled Android apps on an x86 Intel Core Ultra 5 325 chip causes occasional stutters and compatibility hiccups. Even Google seems painfully aware of how rushed this launch build was. Earlier this month, Google pushed an emergency November software update specifically to patch NPU memory leaks and browser tab bloat, freeing up between 3GB and 6GB of RAM on 16GB systems. Needing a day-ten rescue patch just to stop a 16GB laptop from choking on its own AI overhead tells you everything you need to know about how ready Googlebook OS was.

Industry observers note that to be fair, Google did get a few ecosystem touches right. If you carry an Android flagship, initial setup links your phone seamlessly. Features like Continue On app handoff and instant access to your phone's local files feel effortless, showing that Google could one day build desktop-mobile integrations as slick as Apple's Continuity suite. Gemini integration is also thoughtfully executed in spots: Rambler does an impressive job turning rambling voice dictation into clean, structured checklists, and Magic Pointer lets you hover over on-screen text for instant contextual actions.

Sounds great, right? Don’t get too excited: these few redeeming software features cannot rescue an OS that lacks real desktop depth, nor can they fix mediocre endurance. Despite a sizable 70Wh battery, real-world stamina hovers around six to eight hours of mixed work with noticeable overnight standby drain, trailing both Apple silicon and modern Windows ultrabooks by a wide margin. Lenovo already knows how to build laptops with excellent battery life, so I’m putting this firmly on how unoptimized Googlebook OS actually is.

In a recent development, no, not at $1,099. In fact, there’s little reason to buy any Googlebook; the OS is that bad.

If you love the idea of a featherweight 15-inch OLED laptop, buy an ultrabook from Lenovo’s Yoga or Slim lineups, or look at the MacBook Air. Either option will cost you roughly the same money (or less), give you vastly better battery life, and run genuine desktop software without forcing you to compromise your workflow. Conversely, if you live entirely inside Google Workspace and Chrome, buy a Chromebook Plus for half the price and keep $600 in your pocket.

The Lenovo Googlebook 15 is a painful reminder of what happens when hardware and software teams operate on completely different wavelengths. Lenovo revealed up ready to build a flagship laptop; Google showed up with a half-baked mobile OS and expected consumers to pay a premium. Until Google figures out what a desktop operating system actually needs to be, skip this entirely.

It has been revealed that at this point, I doubt it ever will. So don't buy the Lenovo Googlebook 15. Do not buy any Googlebook. Unless something drastic changes with Google’s approach, I probably wouldn't buy any Google desktop computer, ever.
