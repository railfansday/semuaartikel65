According to recent reports, jensen Huang is partying it up in Ibiza. In a video with nearly 6 million views on Instagram, the former CEO of Nvidia is dancing in a crowded club, champagne glass in hand, still wearing his signature black leather jacket. Suddenly, from behind him, Alice Huang — the Chinese technology executive and suspected spy he had an affair with, sparking congressional investigations and his resignation from the chipmaking company he cofounded — appears. She puts her hand on his shoulder and he wraps his arm around her waist as they begin dancing together. The video’s caption reads “Honey Trap Final Boss. #inthemoodforlove.”.

Of course, it’s AI-generated. This Instagram Reel is one of hundreds of AI deepfakes featuring Huang, his wife Lori, Alice, and sometimes Nvidia’s vice president of healthcare, Kimberly Powell (the second of three reported affair partners). In this case, Huang is being swapped into an existing meme called “Ibiza Final Boss.” The original video featured a British construction worker with a bowl cut dancing in exactly the same way, minus a woman sidling up to him. Other viral AI hits include Huang in a scene from The Office, Huang in the music video for George Michael’s “Careless Whisper,” and Huang as the former Astronomer CEO who got caught in an affair with one of his employees by the jumbotron at a Coldplay concert last summer. Even the rapper 50 Cent posted an AI edit of his “P.I.M.P.” music video, now featuring Huang with some flashy silver accessories.

Unlike deepfake pornography or election disinformation, these AI videos aren’t necessarily intended to harm or mislead people. They’re memes making fun of Huang’s cheating scandal and its global implications, created with AI technology that in many cases relies heavily on Nvidia’s tech stack: video diffusion models running on the Nvidia GPUs that power much of the modern generative AI industry. “It’s ironic that without Nvidia generating millions of these AI memes ... would not be possible,” reads one of the top comments on the Ibiza deepfake. “In some sense, Jensen is making Nvidia richer by his own scandal.” The comment was punctuated by multiple cry-laughing emoji.

Recent findings suggest that as it turns out, Huang may benefit, too. On Monday, the Nvidia board unveiled he was reinstated as president, CEO, and board director following “a series of discussions and meetings” and “input from senior levels of the United States government.” Nvidia’s stock has since surged, and latest AI Huang memes cast him in a victorious light. One variation swaps him into a scene from The Wolf of Wall Street, announcing “I’m not leaving” to a crowd of cheering men that has been edited to include AI versions of Donald Trump, Elon Musk, and Mark Zuckerberg.

While the saga continues, many social media consumers are finding the humor in these Huang deepfakes, and their creators are churning out variations to meet the sudden demand. The Instagram account behind the Ibiza one has made three Huang deepfakes so far, all remakes of the same scene. Another version features Powell dancing with him, and the third one shows a sea of young women in bikini tops and shorts surrounding Huang, who lifts his champagne up in celebration. Each has accrued millions of views.

In a recent development, “His story has just come at the best time for ai creators and a terrible time for him,” wrote the account behind the Ibiza videos in a direct message to The Verge. “Everyone was doing him. Most I’ve seen anyone AI-ed.”.

This deepfake creator identified himself as a 40-year-old man from Melbourne, Australia, but wouldn’t share his real name. He states he has spent “thousands” on AI video generation, using models like TikTok developer ByteDance’s Seedance 2.5 and Alibaba’s Wan 3. These, along with other AI tools like Google’s Nano Banana, help power the ecosystem of deepfakes.

Sources indicate that he said his Instagram page has been going “super viral” in the past two weeks, thanks to the Ibiza meme format. He has nearly 100 million views on various iterations of it featuring different celebrities. The Huang ones are among the most popular, alongside versions featuring Elizabeth Holmes and Amanda Seyfried playing her in the hit Nathan Fielder documentary You Can See Everything.

In a recent development, “I wanted to elevate the content and know that putting what is happening in the news ... would get traction,” he wrote. “Always wanted to be a filmmaker but this technology is beyond whatever I imagined was possible.”.

As generative AI has taken hold of the internet in recent years, a lot of the resulting viral content has been disparaged as “AI slop.” Hollywood productions, mainstream artists, and even local businesses have been maligned for using (or appearing to use) AI in their advertisements and final cuts. Video trends like putting sexy AI fruit characters in Love Island parodies racked up millions of views online but failed to deliver any real cultural momentum. The ubiquity of the Huang deepfakes and the relative lack of blowback from AI critics may indicate that the future of AI-generated content is similar to the rest of meme culture: If the topic’s trending, people will engage with something, even if it’s following the same formula as the slop. That an AI tech titan’s own indiscretions are inspiring this latest evolution in deepfakes is just the sardonic cherry on top.

Sources indicate that “It’s not that we’re inundated in deepfakes of Trump being like ‘Nuclear war tomorrow,’ like the way it was framed as the big problem,” states misinformation researcher Abbie Richards. “Instead, it just feels like there is so much slop to push through to have any grounding in reality.”.

Sources indicate that the AI memes of Huang and other public figures don’t feel as sinister as other forms of AI disinformation and misinformation, mainly because of how goofy they are. It’s hard to imagine anyone falling for a “Gangnam Style” parody or any serious implications if they did. But these deepfakes are still eerily realistic, as one comment on the Seyfried Ibiza variation pointed out: “All jokes aside, is anyone else finding it increasingly alarming that AI is capable of this?”.

Sources indicate that even the creator behind the Ibiza AI videos thinks “someone will try and clamp down because it’s getting outta control.” But in the meantime, he’s looking for ways to monetize his newfound virality. Despite his significant financial outlay, he notes he hasn’t made any money yet, besides getting “gifted $3 on a reel once.”.

While Instagram has started to invite some creators to earn direct payouts from views on their Reels, the account behind the AI “Careless Whisper” parody featuring Huang told The Verge that “the way to earn is through creating content for clients and companies after gaining attention on social media.” This AI creator, who declined to share any identifying information, wrote in a direct message that they receive dozens of requests from new and returning clients for AI commissions every time they go viral, “which is quite often.” In addition to Huang deepfakes, some of their most viral AI edits feature Tobey Maguire’s Spider-Man as a rapper.

Sources indicate that the Ibiza AI meme creator has worked with one celebrity brand so far, belonging to the Irish mixed martial artist Conor McGregor. Four months ago, McGregor launched an energy drink called MAC Energy, and the manufacturer requested an AI Ibiza meme showing McGregor dancing with a can of it. The creator complied, and the post exists as a collaboration between the two Instagram pages, but it isn’t labeled as an advertisement and he says he didn’t ask for any money in return.

“I just did it because it was a good opportunity for my page,” he indicates.

In a recent development, it seems like the entity that stands to reap the biggest reward from viral Huang deepfakes is Instagram itself and the other social media sites where creators are posting them. And these humorous AI edits are technically allowed on Meta platforms, as long as creators disclose that they’re AI-generated. Meta didn’t respond to a request for comment by publication time.

Industry observers note that but there are other considerations besides profitability. Many of these AI memes hinge on sexist portrayals and racist stereotypes as the joke. Asian American women in Silicon Valley have reported an uptick in microaggressions revolving around “honeypot” and “Chinese spy” allegations. And when it comes to Powell and the other Nvidia employee who Huang reportedly had a relationship with, the workplace power dynamic at play has faded into the background of mockery.

The more troubling dynamics of Huang’s affairs have faded into the background of mockery.

Sources indicate that “Even though we know that he was wielding his power to have inappropriate relationships with his subordinates and his employees, it’s still like ‘LOL, let’s make an AI deepfake of him at the office,’” states Josh Lora, a writer and creator who has a popular TikTok series about the role of cheating scandals in American culture. “Why aren’t we having a real conversation about the effects of this on women, the effects of this workplace on women, especially 10 years after #MeToo?”.

The apparent lack of interest from major social media companies to stem the flow of this kind of disparaging AI content isn’t surprising to misinformation expert Richards, even as some tech CEOs, like Elon Musk, called for Huang’s reinstatement and cast doubt on the reporting against him. If Huang memes are truly in vogue, that may be worth more to the AI industry than his dignity.

“I think that they are invested in the widespread adoption of AI,” she says. “So anything with AI going viral and appearing to be popular and liked by the public probably, in their mind, benefits them.”.

Sources indicate that with Huang back as CEO, the message couldn’t be clearer: For him, the consequences of his affairs were temporary, including whatever humiliation stemmed from the AI memes. Ultimately, all any of it meant was more power for Nvidia and more power for Jensen Huang.
