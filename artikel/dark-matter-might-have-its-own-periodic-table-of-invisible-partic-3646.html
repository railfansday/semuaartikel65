Dark Matter Might Have Its Own Periodic Table of Invisible Particles

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sources indicate that dark matter accounts for approximately 85 percent of all matter in the universe, though that’s about all we really know about the mysterious substance. Scientists believe it because of the gravitational effects it has on galaxies and other cosmic structures, but since it doesn’t otherwise interact with ordinary matter, it’s been a challenge for scientists to determine what it’s really made of.

It has been revealed that for decades, scientists have been trying to determine whether dark matter consists of a single type of particle or if it’s made up of several particles that could have different properties and thus different interactions with the surrounding world. Earlier this year, scientists found the most convincing evidence of a dark matter particle yet, and if the second scenario is true, it’s possible we could find more.

This second possibility also raises an intriguing question: If there are a variety of “dark particles,” could they interact and combine with one another—just as the particles we know do—to form their own dark atoms and molecules? If that were the case, the dark universe could be much more complex than researchers had initially imagined.

But luckily, humanity has already faced a similar challenge in trying to understand the diversity of ordinary matter. Though to many introductory chemistry students, the periodic table can seem like an odd way to organize information, it’s one of the most ingenious tools developed in our understanding of the physical world. This classification system doesn’t just order the elements, but reveals patterns among their properties that even helped predict the existence of other elements that had not yet been discovered.

In a recent development, now, a team of five physicists is exploring a scenario in which dark matter could form different classes of nuclei and atoms, governed by their own rules. Their research suggests that we may need something similar to a periodic table to understand the organization of dark matter.

Sources indicate that the hypothesis at the heart of this research seems straight out of science fiction. The researchers propose that our universe would contain a kind of “mirror sector,” where particles that barely interact with visible matter have their own forces and can interact with one another.

The idea is not latest and is quite serious. The “Mirror Twin Higgs” was proposed two decades ago as a possible solution to a problem related to the mass of the Higgs boson. According to this theory, mirror particles would help offset certain quantum effects that might otherwise cause its mass to be much greater than what we observe. Although the existence of these particles has never been proven, the proposal offered an ingenious way to address the problem without requiring all new particles to be visible to detectors.

Sources indicate that one of the creators of the Mirror Twin Higgs theory, physicist Zackaria Chacko, is part of this new investigation that extends this hypothesis into the dark matter puzzle. Along with several collaborators, he explores in this recent preprint what would happen if particles from this hypothetical mirror sector could combine with one another, forming nuclei, atoms, and a variety of dark elements.

In a recent development, their calculations suggest that, under certain conditions, these particles could combine to form dark equivalents of some of the elements we know, ranging from the simplest to others as complex as carbon. However, this would not be an exact copy of our matter. The dark particles would have different masses and properties, which would also change the rules governing how they bond, what structures they can form, and which ones remain stable.

In a recent development, for example, in the researchers’ model, dark hydrogen atoms would be unstable and could not survive as those in our universe do. This is a striking difference when we consider that hydrogen is the simplest and most abundant element in ordinary matter.

If these dark elements were ever discovered, understanding the rules governing their formation would be essential for classifying them. A sort of “dark periodic table” could help bring order to that diversity, just as it did with the elements we know.

Sources indicate that for now, this all remains in the realm of theoretical physics. Researchers have not observed any of these atoms, and some doubt such structures could actually form. But there’s plenty about dark matter we have yet to discover.
