Tech Report: AI data center boom strains construction labor across the U.S.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

President Donald Trump mentioned last week that American companies will face a "tremendous tax" for building data centers outside of the U.S.

According to recent reports, "We have a lot of places that want them badly, and they're great places. They have energy, and they have everything," Trump said at the end of that week's major tech stock selloff.

Amid the nationwide public backlash against data centers and the ongoing market turmoil affecting the artificial intelligence industry itself, there's a more pressing problem with Trump's demand — a worker shortage.

Across the country, tech companies are already competing for a limited pool of electricians, plumbers and other skilled construction workers needed to erect the massive facilities powering the AI boom. Associated Builders and Contractors (ABC) estimates the construction industry needs to attract 349,000 net new workers in 2026 just to keep pace with demand.

Recent findings suggest that the pressure is hitting home in Abilene, Texas, where thousands of workers have descended on the region to build massive data center projects. RV parks and temporary housing have expanded to accommodate the influx, while local homebuilders and contractors say they're losing skilled workers to data center jobs offering substantially higher pay.

For Abilene homebuilder Gene Lantrip, the boom has become what he calls "a two-edged sword." Data centers are bringing fresh business and residents to the city, while making it harder and more expensive to find the workers.

Sources indicate that skilled laborers have been flocking to Texas in droves since 2025 to construct massive data center facilities like the $500 billion Stargate Project for OpenAI, Oracle and Japan's SoftBank. The vast prairie surrounding Abilene is now adorned with mile-wide construction sites and a collection of RV parks and mobile homes to accommodate the region's new blue-collar residents.

Sources indicate that tech giants have pitched these gigantic data center facilities as necessary to train and run powerful foundation models. And because these tech firms want data center campuses to come online quickly, they've been luring construction workers around the U.S. with lavish pay packages to settle down in rural places like Richland Parish, Louisiana, which will be home to Meta's over $50 billion Hyperion data center.

Industry observers note that lantrip said "the economy of Abilene is great because of the data center" and he's busier than ever building houses to accommodate the multitude of newly introduced residents. But the overall cost and time to build homes is increasing due in part to having a smaller pool of workers to hire from, he said.

According to recent reports, scotty Wristen, an Abilene master electrician who works with Lantrip, said that some of his electricians decamped to work on AI data centers because of the better pay. Even the local commercial electrical companies in the area have lost workers to the AI data centers, he said.

Industry observers note that "I know of one manufacturer that lost 42 guys in one day," Wristen stated. "If I was young and didn't have a wife and kids, I would probably do it too, because they pay at least triple what I can pay.".

Those jobs also include on-the-job training, sponsored by the tech companies.

To boost the supply of construction workers, tech giants like Meta, Google and Amazon are spending millions of dollars on training programs that place aspiring builders at data center sites for hands-on, paid experience. The ABC is at the moment partnering with Meta on the social media giant's $115 million American Workforce Academy, pitched as "a nationwide, unprecedented fast-track to a long-term career in a skilled trade.".

Meta's training program has so far "attracted thousands of folks who want to get into construction and learn a trade and to eventually work in data centers," said Joel Thames, an ABC vice president of health safety, environment and workforce development.It's unclear what happens to towns like Abilene and the throng of data center construction workers once they finish building the facilities. Tech companies will have a tougher time initiating latest data center buildouts in the U.S. following the midterm elections in which voters overwhelmingly supported various restrictions on data center related projects.

In a recent development, texas Democratic Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa has called for a statewide data center moratorium until additional rules are in place giving residents more agency to determine whether newly introduced facilities should be built. For data center real estate developers like Anita Verma-Lallian, who has projects in Texas and Arizona, any restrictions placed on new initiatives may speed up the completion of the existing buildout.

"What's happened in my view, it's just going to make the demand even higher because the supply is going to be so much more constrained," Verma-Lallian, the CEO of Arizona Land Consulting, noted. "We're going to see a rush to get those projects built and online as soon as possible."Verma-Lallian said that there is "going to be some uncertainty" for potential newly introduced data center projects in Texas due to any yet-to-be-known restrictions. She views Arizona as being a more favorable environment to data center development and noted that Democratic incumbent Governor Katie Hobbs is "fairly moderate on data centers." If data center construction eventually slows in places like Texas, Verma-Lallian said it's possible that the new labor diaspora "will go to other states or other projects.".
