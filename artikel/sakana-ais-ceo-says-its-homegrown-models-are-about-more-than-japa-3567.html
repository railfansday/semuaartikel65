Sakana AI’s CEO says its homegrown models are about more than Japan’s national security

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Sakana AI, a Japanese AI startup, last month revealed prepares to develop its own foundation model. The move has drawn attention for its potential impact on Japan’s defense and cybersecurity sectors as well as concerns about reliance on U.S. and Chinese technologies.

Sakana AI’s co-founder and CEO, David Ha, told TechCrunch that its upcoming foundation model, Sawara, is part of the organization’s broader goal of supporting and strengthening Japan’s industrial and manufacturing sectors using AI. In addition, Ha noted rising cybersecurity threats and stressed the need for domestic AI alternatives for sensitive applications, particularly if access to foreign models becomes limited.

“When we started planning for this model, of course, we had physical AI, manufacturing, and industry in mind, and we still do. This is our long-term vision,” Ha said. “But short-term, I think Japan has a real need for security-related capabilities as well.”.

Sakana will begin working on Sawara this month, aiming to train and release a smaller-scale model in early 2027, followed by a larger version.

The project comes roughly five months after Sakana launched Fugu, an AI orchestration system that ropes in other large language models, including those developed by U.S. AI labs, to assemble AI agents according to the task at hand. The Tokyo-based startup has also built Namazu, a Japanese-focused language model that fine-tunes existing open-weight models from Meta and DeepSeek to better understand Japanese language, culture, and business needs, including translation, political questions, and AI agent tasks.

Sources indicate that “We cannot simply use open-weight models as-is in Japan for enterprises because they’re usually biased towards their creators,” Ha said. “They may refuse to answer questions about Japan or Japanese politics […] they may give Japanese people answers they don’t want to hear.”.

However, Ha acknowledged that developing a domestic foundation model alone will not fully eliminate reliance on foreign AI technology.

In a recent development, “The only thing [left] for us to become a full-stack firm is the pre-training, Sawara,” Ha continued. “We blueprint to apply the [Sawara] technology in industry and manufacturing. We’ll train both a small and a large model next year. The small model should be particularly useful for on-premises solutions. Japan needs on-premises deployment capabilities.”.

Recent findings suggest that while Fugu incorporates OpenAI’s models, Ha noted that foreign AI models are not enough for certain applications. For example, in cybersecurity, Japan requires that customized models be deployed on-premises.

In a recent development, “Japan will continue to use U.S. systems, but it also wants domestic alternatives,” Ha remarked. “People often point to Palantir and other systems. I have a great deal of respect for companies such as Snowflake, Palantir, and Datadog. But for national defense, Japan will want its own systems.”.

Sakana signed a research contract with Japan’s Ministry of Defense in July to explore how AI could help analyze and integrate information from multiple sources, and it has also worked with the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications, on systems for detecting disinformation.

Sakana’s work with Japanese government agencies has shaped its approach to developing Sawara, Ha remarked. Unlike commercial customers, government agencies often have stricter requirements, including the ability to audit training data, customize AI models and deploy them on-premises.

“Those lessons helped us plan how to develop and deploy Sawara. I think similar requirements exist in many governments,” he said.

While enterprise customers can use a combination of Fugu, Namazu and AI models developed overseas, Sakana’s government-related work at the moment focuses on two main areas: disinformation detection and analysis, and multimodal systems that integrate data from different government sources.

Sakana’s expansion into developing its own foundation models comes amid speculation about a potential IPO following reports by Nikkei and Bloomberg earlier this month.

But Ha remarked the organization doesn’t have any near-term plans to go public.

The reported timing and potential valuation were speculative, Ha said, adding that he did not know when the company might pursue a listing, though he expects his startup to eventually go public in Japan itself.

“We have a gravitational pull to eventually IPO in Japan,” Ha remarked.

It has been revealed that ha sees Sakana’s future growth tied to the global expansion of Japanese manufacturers. Rather than focusing on selling its models, the tech giant wants to help local companies use AI to become more competitive globally.

Industry observers note that “We want to strategically work with the government and Japanese industrial base, make them competitive,” Ha remarked. “We want to go abroad, go international together with manufacturing and industrial sectors.”.
