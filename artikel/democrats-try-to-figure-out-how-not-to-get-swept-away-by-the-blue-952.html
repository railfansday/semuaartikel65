Democrats try to figure out how not to get swept away by the blue wave

On a harried Saturday afternoon at Denver International Airport, I had the good fortune of sitting just a few feet from Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey. It shouldn’t have come as a surprise. He, I, and 3,500 or so others had just left “What’s Next,” a conference organized by Majority Democrats, a group dedicated to charting a path for the suddenly — and shockingly? — victorious party.

Frey, who rose to national prominence amid ICE’s brutal Operation Metro Surge, had come to the conference in Denver to listen, not to speak. “I’ve never been to one of these things before,” he stated. He had flown in for a day and, like everyone else in attendance, is curious to see where the party goes from here. He said he hopes Democrats do two things with their newfound power. “One, act with urgency. And second, don’t fuck it up.”.

Another conference-goer sitting nearby chimed in: “Abso-fucking-lutely.”.

In a recent development, democrats, unaccustomed to winning, have seemingly learned from the folly of Republicans’ 2024 sweep. Where Donald Trump’s return to power was falsely attributed to a vibe shift of seismic proportions, born of voters’ preoccupation over DEI and desire to say slurs, Majority Democrats has a simpler explanation for the blue tsunami: It’s the economy, stupid. Everything is falling apart, and even just getting by feels more expensive than ever. The midterms were a rejection, not an endorsement: Voters rejected data centers, they rejected war, and they cast their lot with the side that promised to give them what they want.

The Democrats understand this is not a cultural victory. Even the hollow center of the Democratic Party has, at long last, realized voters don’t necessarily want what they’re selling — they just want to afford to live. It’s gas and groceries all the way down. This analysis would almost verge on Marxist if it wasn’t put forth by an organization comprising the party’s newly introduced, young moderates. Majority Democrats describes itself as an alternative to three unpopular, ineffective factions: the “gerontocratic establishment,” the “activist left,” and the “MAGA right.”.

Industry observers note that the Democrats want you to know they’ve changed. This is no longer the party of “Kamala is brat” or “Pokémon Go to the polls.” They are, in fact, “A Different Kind of Democrat.” They’ve abandoned hollow identity politics and sworn off toxic factional infighting. They really, really care about affordability — for real. They even have the merch to prove it.

Attendees at What’s Next wore their convictions on their sleeves, and also their chests, and also their heads. There were hats that read “pay your taxes” and hats identifying the wearer as a “upcoming deal democrat,” both in all lowercase because the Democrats would also like to remind you that they are once again the youth party. At a separate merch stand, there was a T-shirt declaring “America!” and another T-shirt posing an existential question, also in all lowercase: “how did fucking computers become the thing and machines became the thing that does art? and humans are like the ones in amazon warehouses like shouldn’t robots be working so we can paint?” This second shirt, a friend pointed out, is an uncredited reference to a joke alt-comedian Stavros Halkias made on Theo Von’s podcast. And there was the Our Get Along Shirt, a sartorial exercise in magical thinking that summed up the ethos of the What’s Next conference. The Democrats were in control. Having realized the folly of fighting each other, they would instead channel their energy into fighting for the American people.

The illusion of harmony was shattered early into the programming when a small group of dissenters heckled Rep. Hakeem Jeffries of New York, the soon-to-be speaker of the House. “We’re asking you to listen to us now,” one of the protesters shouted after the moderator, Fox News’s resident Democratic host Jessica Tarlov, attempted to placate them by saying she’d be asking Jeffries about the youth vote. “When is the time for a Green New Deal? When is the time for an arms embargo?” The protesters’ cries were drowned out by the crowd’s chants of “HA-KEEM, HA-KEEM, HA-KEEM.”.

It has been revealed that jeffries, ever the politician, was unfazed by the altercation. “I’m from Brooklyn,” he told his visibly uncomfortable interlocutor. “This is light work.” The protesters were whisked away, and the interview continued as if the interruption had never happened. Democrats would be gracious in victory.

This new way of doing things was best summed up by Michigan Sen. Elissa Slotkin, who told reporters that she and her new colleague, Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, had plenty of debates in the car en route to campaign events — though she declined to say what the debates were about. (Slotkin has identified as a Zionist but has also voted against providing certain military aid to Israel. El-Sayed has called Israel’s war on Gaza a genocide.) “Abdul and I do not agree on everything, but we agree on the really major, important thing, and that is making sure we have a middle class in this country,” she stated. “When I see elected officials spending their time fighting the progressive-versus-moderate battle, I know that person didn’t actually learn a damn thing from the election.”.

It has been revealed that throughout the weekend, speaker after speaker urged Democrats not to squander their newfound control of Congress by bickering among themselves. At the airport, notorious Democratic operative Lis Smith, an adviser to Majority Democrats, told me the midterms sweep was a rejection of Trumpism, not a sign that voters prefer one style of Democratic politics over the other. “A big takeaway of the election was that there’s no one way to be a Democrat, and that’s in part how we were able to have such a big victory,” she stated. The calls for unity were reminiscent of the slogan of one doomed 2016 presidential campaign: Stronger Together. This time, however, they may be onto something — at least for now.

In a recent development, “It’s easy to have Pete Buttigieg and Ro Khanna onstage when we’re all riding the same high,” one staffer told me on the final night of the conference. But what happens when it’s actually time to govern?

For all the talk of unity, there was plenty of tension at What’s Next — sometimes overt, more often simmering beneath the surface of what organizers described as a congenial event.

Recent findings suggest that there was tension toward the establishment, as characterized by Maryland Sen. Chris Van Hollen’s announcement that he was challenging Sen. Chuck Schumer (D-NY) for Senate majority leader. There was tension between speakers’ repeated exhortations to get “major money” out of politics and the fact that Majority Democrats is largely bankrolled by hedge fund billionaire Stephen Mandel, a major investor in companies powering the AI boom. (Several attendees I spoke to described What’s Next as hands down the most luxe Democratic event they’ve been to, on par with the party’s national conventions. The snack station was stocked with full-size candy bars.) There was tension between all the populist posturing and the nightly afterparties at the Ritz, where 2028 contenders, staffers, and hangers-on of all types drank well after last call. And notwithstanding the recent blue tsunami, there was tension between Democrats’ persistent unlikability and the conference’s poptimism-tinged branding.

It has been revealed that everything was a little too cute. Majority Democrats staffers wore custom Dickies-style jackets with their names embroidered on the front. Highlight reels played between each main stage speech. The opening-night happy hour, sponsored by the Searchlight Institute — a think tank founded in 2025 with the stated goal of weakening the influence of liberal groups like the ACLU and the Center for American Progress — was Western-themed, though hardly any attendees strayed from the blazer-and-lanyard dress code that is de rigueur in liberal policy circles. The conference lineup was confirmed via a music festival-style flyer. The halls were decorated with stylized posters of the politicians in attendance, many of whom seemed to be soft-launching their 2028 presidential campaigns.

According to recent reports, “The organizers here want a substantial tent — one big enough even to invite anti-establishment voices like me,” Khanna, one apparent presidential aspirant, mentioned from the stage. “I’m all for a big tent. I’m not for a shallow one. A big tent cannot mean that we water down principle for political expediency.” He called on the new Democratic-controlled Congress to pass Medicare for All, codify a path to citizenship for undocumented immigrants, abolish ICE, and cut off funding to Israel, which he said was committing an ongoing genocide against Palestinians. All of this got huge cheers from the audience — some of whom, one presumes, also applauded Jeffries just one day earlier.

Depending on whom you ask, What’s Next was either a concerted effort to rally Democrats around an affordability agenda or moderates’ cynical attempt at co-opting voters’ populist rage. Across the street from the Mission Ballroom, a small group of protesters with Indivisible unfurled a giant banner that read “NO SCHUMER CORONATION.” Members of another group, posted up by one of the side stages hosting the “Do Issues Matter?” panel, told me they hoped the Democrats didn’t abandon their commitment to immigration reform and abortion rights. At the “AI safety breakfast,” one advocate of tech regulation told me there was little daylight between the organizations putting on the conference and its affiliated events — Majority Democrats, the Searchlight Institute, and the pro-AI regulation PAC Tomorrow Together — and the flailing Abundance movement. “They’re rebranding because no one likes abundance,” she noted. “This is counter-programming to the populist left. They took a bunch of AI safety money to put on things like this.”.

Recent findings suggest that there were notable absences. Vermont senator and erstwhile presidential candidate Bernie Sanders was not in attendance, and neither was New York Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, whom the New York City chapter of the Democratic Socialists of America recently urged to run for president. Slotkin may have name-checked El-Sayed, but he wasn’t there, either. Not even Colorado Rep.-elect Melat Kiros – a member of the Democratic Socialists of America who won her race in a landslide and now represents the very district where the conference was held – was present. Rep. Jake Auchincloss, a member of Majority Democrats who helped organize the event, told The Washington Post that he had personally invited Ocasio-Cortez.

Sources indicate that still, there were other representatives of the party’s left wing milling about, including Khanna, Van Hollen, and Angie Nixon, a DSA-backed Democrat who came within 3 percentage points of winning a Florida Senate seat. And regardless of their political leanings — and counter to the Democratic Party’s 2024 message that the economy was just fine, actually — nearly every single speaker decried the steadily increasing economic divide separating America’s billionaires from the shrinking middle class and the growing ranks of the working poor.

It has been revealed that “We just won in a historic fashion, and I think everyone feels good about that,” remarked Morris Katz, a 26-year-old digital strategist whose roster of previous clients includes Latest York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Texas Gov.-elect Gina Hinojosa. (Katz was also the driving force behind Senate contender Graham Platner, who dropped out of the race after being accused of sexual assault.) “I think everyone is united under an anti-billionaire focus on affordability and corruption, and there’s a lot to feel good about.”.

And what about the billionaire funding this very conference?

In a recent development, “If there are people who want to support where we’re headed, and the things we’re doing, great, everyone’s welcome,” Katz told me. The difference, he said, is whether Democrats let themselves be co-opted or corrupted by their donors. He pointed to outgoing Texas Republican Gov. Greg Abbott — who supercharged tech development in the state and whose campaign was largely funded by Elon Musk and data center developers — as an example of what could go wrong. “There’s a quid pro quo there that needs to be avoided at all costs. We need to be the anti-Substantial Tech party.”.

Democrats are already heading in that direction. Khanna pointed out that one-third of the nation’s wealth is concentrated in the Silicon Valley district he represents. Jerusalem Demsas, founder of the liberal publication The Argument, urged legislators to rein in AI development. (“I know everyone here is in a good mood, and it’s about to not be a good mood,” she stated before reminding the assembled legislators and political operatives that “what comes after winning is governing.”) At a press conference after her speech, Slotkin called for the establishment of a bipartisan, bicameral committee on AI akin to midcentury bodies created to regulate nuclear power. “While we’re bickering with each other — Democrats and Republicans — there’s an opportunity cost, and we have not done what we need to do on AI,” she said. “If we were healthy politically, we would have been working on this for five years straight.”.

Industry observers note that there was an obvious undercurrent of technopessimism throughout the weekend. News of the nationwide FAA ground stop broke minutes before a panel titled “How Weird Will AI Make 2028?” Though a faulty router was ultimately identified as the culprit, attendees — myself included — couldn’t help but speculate whether an AI cyberattack, or even just shoddy implementation of the FAA’s newly introduced AI tool, was actually to blame.

According to recent reports, “It was clear that there is just a huge interest in AI here, and grappling with it, that sits across the party,” Rob Flaherty, the cofounder of the AI safety PAC Tomorrow Together told me. Flaherty’s organization hosted an off-the-record breakfast on AI safety. “We built it for 100 people, and 600 people revealed up,” he said. The coffee ran out almost immediately, though there were somehow enough burritos to go around. Tomorrow Together’s polling, which was shared at the breakfast, indicates that voters don’t just hate data centers but have broadly soured on AI. They tie AI developments to broader economic concerns like rising utility bills and a worsening job market. Most importantly, voters don’t think the companies can be trusted to regulate themselves.

“Now it’s on Democrats to not just channel the anger and be the vessel for that anger but to actually enact the things that are going to alleviate the pain that people are feeling,” Flaherty told me.

Recent findings suggest that but for Flaherty, who previously worked for Biden’s and Harris’ presidential campaigns, the conference “was less about a policy agenda and more about a theory of power” — one articulated by Bernie Sanders a decade ago. “If you look at the way people — James Talarico on down, Hakeem Jeffries on down — were speaking, it doesn’t sound dissimilar from the kinds of things Bernie was raising in 2016,” said Flaherty. “He rightly — and I was working on the other side — identified some of the economic discontent that a lot of people are feeling.”.

Still, there’s a difference between Sanders’ vision of Medicare for All and, say, a public option — or what 2020 presidential hopeful Pete Buttigieg described as “Medicare for all who want it.” Both fall under the broad umbrella of universal healthcare, but the distinctions matter. Absent nitty-gritty policy discussions, however, it’s easy to get the sense that all voters — and all Democrats — want the same thing. Perhaps this is why, even among the professional Democrats in attendance, I met people who told me they wanted Democrats to prosecute ICE agents, enact a $25 an hour minimum wage, and pack the Supreme Court. At a certain point, I became desperate to find someone whose politics were a little more tepid.

Recent findings suggest that my pleas were answered by Uriel Espinoza, a local Democrat whose friends introduced him as a centrist. Espinoza’s friends insisted that I join them for happy hour at a nearby brewery. (Espinoza noted he rejects the centrist label, but broadly disagrees with the Democratic Socialists of America’s manifesto.) Their car wasn’t big enough to fit us all, but it didn’t matter. As four of us piled into the back seat of a Tesla whose driver — another member of the friend group — was blasting Drake, Espinoza told me he had once identified as a conservative. At the bar, he and another friend said the conference had been fun, if a little “East Coast consultant-y.”.

According to recent reports, here was the mythical moderate voter in all his glory. He was even wearing a cowboy hat. And what did he want? Immigration reform, universal healthcare, and an economy that isn’t stacked against working people. There was a time, Espinoza told me, when he felt betrayed. “On certain things — like Israel, Gaza, Palestine, immigration — we are looking to our elected officials at the highest level, and we feel that they aren’t really delivering, or differentiating himself from Republicans,” he mentioned.

I told him he sounded almost like a leftist.

“I’m also surprised, myself, because I’ve never thought of myself — and I don’t think of myself — as a socialist,” he told me. “I’m an entrepreneur. I’m pro-business, I’m pro-economy, but I’m also, at this point, pro-results.”.
