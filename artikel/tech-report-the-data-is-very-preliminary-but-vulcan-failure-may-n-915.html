Tech Report: The data is very preliminary, but Vulcan failure may not be due to BE-4 engines

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

United Launch Alliance (ULA) confirmed Tuesday morning that its return-to-flight launch of the Vulcan rocket began failing during the initial stages of the mission.

“While we appear to have lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight, early indications are that just after liftoff, the launch vehicle experienced low booster performance, resulting in stage separation occurring when the vehicle was low in altitude and below minimal velocity to reach orbit,” the manufacturer noted.

The launch company also confirmed reporting by Ars that the Centaur V upper stage and the solid rocket boosters performed nominally during the flight.

According to recent reports, critically, ULA did not provide any specific information about the “low booster performance.” This matters greatly because the core stage of the Vulcan rocket is powered by two BE-4 engines, which are provided by Blue Origin. That company’s own Newly introduced Glenn rocket uses a similar but slightly more powerful version of the BE-4 engine. And New Glenn experienced its own catastrophic failure in May during a ground test, a failure that later was attributed to a main oxygen valve on one of the BE-4 engines.

Industry observers note that information about the Vulcan failure is being closely held by ULA and Blue Origin, who have only acknowledged that they’re working together to investigate the cause. According to two sources, the investigation is “incredibly complicated” and remains in the early stages.

Industry observers note that however, according to preliminary data, two independent sources indicated to Ars that the problem is currently believed to have originated on the launch vehicle side rather than in the engines themselves. The problem may be related to systems that feed methane and liquid oxygen propellant to the engines.

Industry observers note that ULA engineers are looking at the possibility that one of the two BE-4 engines started producing lower thrust shortly after liftoff and never ramped back up to full throttle for the remainder of the burn. Video observations from high-zoom tracking cameras appeared to show one engine slightly dimmer than the other after Vulcan jettisoned its solid rocket boosters nearly two minutes into flight. An asymmetric shutdown of the BE-4 engines could also have contributed to the sudden attitude motion indicated by ULA’s telemetry-driven real-time animation near stage separation.

The Federal Aviation Administration is overseeing the investigation, which is being led by ULA. Again, this information is preliminary, and no definitive conclusions can or should be drawn.

According to recent reports, this was the fifth flight of the Vulcan rocket since its debut in January 2024. Two of the previous flights have experienced problems with the nozzles on its solid rocket boosters, but in both cases, the rocket was able to compensate for the reduced and asymmetric thrust and reach its intended orbit. The second nozzle matter caused Vulcan to stand down for nine months.

Industry observers note that this latest setback is likely to sideline Vulcan for at least half a year, and probably longer. Most impacted will be the US Space Force, which has national security payloads waiting for a ride to space on Vulcan, and Amazon, which had more than a dozen debuts of its Amazon Leo constellation on the manifest for 2027. It would exacerbate a worsening launch crisis in the United States.

Sources indicate that there are potentially serious implications for Blue Origin as well, which is seeking to return Newly introduced Glenn to flight as early as January. Last Tuesday, the manufacturer stated a modified BE-4 main oxygen valve had successfully completed qualification testing.

It has been revealed that “Our modified valve has already been incorporated into engines slated for upcoming flights, and we are now rolling it out across the remaining fleet,” the organization said. “We expect to complete the anomaly investigation later this month.”.

If Vulcan’s failure were ultimately due to a problem with the BE-4 engine, it would certainly delay the Latest Glenn rocket’s return to flight. This would be a setback not just for Blue Origin but for NASA, which is counting on the super heavy lift rocket to do much of the heavy lifting for its Artemis Program and Moon Base.
