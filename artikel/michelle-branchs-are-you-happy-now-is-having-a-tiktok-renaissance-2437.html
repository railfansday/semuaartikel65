Michelle Branch has a question for Gen Z: Are you happy now?

Industry observers note that more than two decades after its release, the singer-songwriter's 2003 breakup anthem "Are You Happy Now?" is finding a fresh audience on TikTok, thanks to a particularly memorable needle drop in Netflix's buzzy teen horror drama The Body. And for millennials who grew up screaming along to the song in their bedrooms, the sudden resurgence feels like a long-overdue moment of recognition — and another painful reminder that we're all old AF.

Welcome to the Michelle Branch renaissance. We've been expecting you.

The song appears in the third episode of The Body, a psychological horror series set at a Catholic girls' school in 2004, where a dance team initiation spirals into a supernatural panic. The show's early-aughts setting provides plenty of opportunities for nostalgic needle drops, with a soundtrack featuring Kelly Clarkson, Ciara, Kylie Minogue, and No Doubt. But Branch's angsty pop-rock hit gets one of the show's most memorable musical moments.

According to recent reports, during a church service, Elise puts on a convincing display of demonic possession, collapsing as the song builds toward its explosive chorus. The juxtaposition of religious imagery, teenage hysteria, and Branch's soaring vocals makes for a scene practically engineered to go viral.

And that's exactly what's happening on social media.

Originally released in 2003 as the lead single from Branch's supremely underrated sophomore album, Hotel Paper, "Are You Happy Now?" became one of the defining songs of the early-2000s pop-rock boom. With its crunchy guitars, emotionally charged vocals, and deliciously confrontational chorus, the track captured a particular kind of teenage heartbreak — the kind that demands to be felt at full volume.

In a recent development, it was a time when young women like Branch, Avril Lavigne, and Vanessa Carlton were helping shape the sound of mainstream pop, bringing guitars, messy emotions, and confessional songwriting to a generation of listeners. They embraced teenage melodrama, not shied away from it.

According to recent reports, now, more than 20 years later, that sound is resonating with a new generation. Just look at the comments on the song's YouTube music video, where the top comment asks, "Who's here after The Body?".

On TikTok, younger listeners are discovering the cathartic power of Branch's early-aughts angst. One user declared it the new background song to their life, while another thanked The Body for introducing them to this "cunty asf song." Others are already looking to expand their musical horizons. "I've been obsessed with this song since The Body," one user wrote, asking for recommendations for artists with a similar sound.

But the nostalgia goes beyond the music itself. One TikToker lamented being born in 2004 instead of getting to experience the year as a teenager, adding, "God I'm SO jealous of millennials." Another praised the show's music supervisor with a particularly heartfelt blessing: "Whoever put this soundtrack together, I hope both sides of your pillow are cold cause wow, you get it.".

The resurgence is part of a familiar pattern in which television and social media introduce younger audiences to older music. Kate Bush's "Running Up That Hill" famously experienced a massive revival after appearing in Stranger Things, while TikTok has repeatedly turned decades-old tracks into contemporary hits.

Industry observers note that what makes "Are You Happy Now?" particularly well-suited to this moment is that its emotional intensity never really went out of style. The song's combination of wounded pride, anger, and catharsis translates just as easily to a TikTok edit as it did to a burned CD in 2003.

For Gen Z, it might be a latest discovery. For millennials, it's confirmation of something we've known all along: Michelle Branch was always that girl.

Sources indicate that for a generation that grew up online, the early 2000s have become something of a cultural fantasy. From Y2K fashion and digital cameras to wired headphones and the resurgence of pop-rock, Gen Z has embraced the aesthetics of a time when the internet still seemed full of possibility. More recently, the "millennial optimism" trend has romanticized the late 2000s and early 2010s, when Tumblr aesthetics, twee fashion, and earnest indie music dominated the internet. It's quite the turnaround for a generation that once made mocking millennial cringe something of a pastime.

But millennial optimism is about more than nostalgia for a particular aesthetic. It's a longing for a time when the future seemed brighter, when hope and sincerity felt less embarrassing, and when pop culture wasn't quite so steeped in irony. Of course, the past wasn't nearly as carefree as nostalgia might suggest. Still, there's something appealing about the emotional earnestness of that era, especially its music.

Sources indicate that and while "Are You Happy Now?" might not exactly embody millennial optimism, its resurgence taps into that same longing for a seemingly simpler cultural moment. The platforms might have changed how we discover music, but the desire to scream along to a song that understands exactly how you feel hasn't changed much at all.

Next up: Justice for Carlton's "White Houses.".
