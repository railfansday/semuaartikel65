Where should you place your Wi-Fi router for the best internet?

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Don't reduce your router's capabilities by stuffing it into a cabinet in the corner of the room.

A weak Wi-Fi signal isn't always a sign of your router going bad or ISP issues. More often than not, it's an indication of a placement problem. Wi-Fi routers broadcast signals in all directions, but the signal weakens over distance, so where you place your router affects the signal quality that reaches your devices. A router stuffed into a corner will likely send much of its signal outside the house where no one needs it. There's also the fact that other electronics can interfere, degrading signal quality simply by being too close.

In a recent development, the best spot for your Wi-Fi router differs depending on your building and where you want it to cover. For example, if you're covering an apartment, the best place for your Wi-Fi router will likely be somewhere that's central and away from electronics that weaken signals.

First, find a central and elevated spot in your home, away from sources of interference, so the router can evenly distribute signal across your space. This could be a side table, desk or even the top of a cabinet. If need be, consider making or buying a stand for the router. Next, keep the router in the open and away from corners, so the signal doesn't wastefully broadcast outside or get blocked.

In a recent development, if your house has two stories, you should move the router closer to the ceiling if it's on the ground floor, or closer to the floor if it's on the second level. Your router's antenna positioning matters too. For single floors or apartments, vertical antenna placement is best. For buildings with multiple floors, angle at least one antenna to about 30 degrees, then keep the others vertical.

Recent findings suggest that before you settle for a position, you'll need to run speed tests to make sure you've picked the best spot. Run the first test before you start adjusting your router to get an idea of its current performance. Next, run tests while you're adjusting the router's position so you can get a clear read on how each position affects your signal quality and internet speed.

According to recent reports, to get the most out of your router, you'll need to minimize interference. Keep your router away from item that interfere with Wi-Fi signals, including metal objects, thick walls (especially metal and concrete), Bluetooth devices and microwaves, which interfere with your router's 2.4GHz band.

Keep in mind that the 2.4GHz band has a longer signal range but slower speeds, while the 5GHz band has a shorter range with faster speeds. So connect devices where speed and stability matter most, like desktop PCs and gaming consoles, to the 5GHz band. Then keep devices that are far away, move a lot or don't need a ton of speed on the 2.4GHz band.

Sometimes, no matter how well you position the router, it still won't cover dead zones. This is especially true for large buildings, or those with three or more floors. In that case, you'll need a Wi-Fi booster (often called a Wi-Fi extender or repeater) or a mesh network.

The best Wi-Fi extenders boost your router's signal coverage by capturing said signal and repeating it to nearby spaces. But this usually comes at the cost of halving your bandwidth, and getting the right Wi-Fi extender placement can be tricky in large spaces. It's a good solution if you want something that's cheap and easy to set up, and don't have a large space to cover. But extenders are often a pain.

Sources indicate that top mesh systems solve the same problem and offer superior performance compared to extenders, but are more expensive. Unlike extenders, mesh systems use nodes that you place in dead zones to extend coverage while keeping everything on a single network. And tri-band systems avoid the pitfall of halving your bandwidth. Mesh systems are a better choice for larger buildings, multi-story homes, and spaces with several dead zones where a single extender isn't enough.

How Do Digital Photo Frames Work?

Digital photo frames collect your photos, usually through an app but also through email and sometimes text, and display them onto the digital screen. They're created to look like a real frame, but instead show off hundreds or thousands of photos without you needing to reframe anything or print every single picture. Our favorite picks all have an app with storage, so they use Wi-Fi to connect the photos to the frame, but some frames also have physical storage on the device to store and remember the pictures.

What Storage Options Are There on Digital Photo Frames?

According to recent reports, all the main frames we recommend use cloud-based storage, so you'll upload your photos to a cloud server for the frame to reach and display them. Aura has unlimited storage for photos, while Nixplay charges $30 a year for unlimited photo storage (along with other features). While both use cloud storage, these are both reputable companies with defined privacy and security practices. Some of the cheaper frames you'll find won't have these processes in place.

Sources indicate that some frames have internal storage that can be expanded with SD cards or even use a USB stick. These options can be more secure, especially if you're moving photos yourself from the computer to the USB or SD card and then connecting it to the frame, but it’s also more work.

Do Smart Displays Work as Digital Photo Frames?

If you already have a smart display, you can set it to cycle through your photo albums when it's idle. How Does Privacy and Security Come Into Play With Digital Photo Frames?

You might not think that photos count as sensitive information, compared to a bank account statement or your Social Security number. But it can be devastating to find photos of loved ones used for nefarious or unsavory purposes.

This is why we prefer frames from reputable companies like Aura and Nixplay over cheaper ones with less clearly defined privacy and security practices. Nixplay offers reasonable security measures, like encrypting your photos during transmission, and Aura offers the option to delete metadata, like the location where a photo was captured. However, both say they may disclose personal information if court-ordered or subpoenaed, and neither provides safety guarantees against hacking.

If you're concerned about security, you can avoid connecting to third-party services, but your most secure option is to just download several photos on a tablet and disable all internet connectivity.

As the center of your setup, capable of receiving signals from all directions, you'd think you can place your smart home hub anywhere, but it's not as simple as that.

Recent findings suggest that many hubs feature controls for all of your connected devices, so it makes sense that you'd keep them in a central spot. But if you control your devices from separate apps or just don't need to make manual adjustments often, it can be tempting to hide the hub out of sight. But since it needs to send and receive signals from all your home's devices, it's important to ensure they aren't blocked or being impeded.

Recent findings suggest that while not all smart home devices run on Wi-Fi, it's helpful to treat the hub the way you would a Wi-Fi router. The most crucial part of placing a smart home hub like Anker's MindBase is making sure signals aren't being physically obstructed. Wi-Fi and other forms of wireless communication can be blocked by walls, furniture and doors, so try to keep as clear a path as possible between the hub and the devices communicating with it. Because you're likely controlling devices throughout your home, some will inevitably have a weaker connection than others, but placing the hub in a central place will help minimize problems. If your hub has visible antennas, try to also point them toward your smart home devices.

Hubs can also experience interference from other communication devices, like Wi-Fi routers. It's impossible to completely eliminate this kind of interference, especially if you're living somewhere with a lot of Wi-Fi traffic, like an apartment building. You should place your hub close enough to a router so that it's still receiving a strong signal, but they should never be right next to each other to avoid interference. Modern routers have settings that allow them to change to less crowded frequencies, and turning that on or setting your router and hub to different bands can help cut down on congestion as well. Other electronics like phones and microwaves can also interfere with smart home hubs, and the only solution here is to keep them away from each other.

If you're limited on placement options, you can create a linked network for Zigbee or ZWave connections to travel to the hub. Keep in mind that the signal they use has a fairly short range. Because these devices form a mesh network, each device repeats the signal it receives, letting you form a chain of devices through your home. That means that the signal from your hub only needs to reach one other device to be effective, but the signal does get weaker across multiple devices. So, even for a network that branches across devices, the closer to the hub a device is, the better.
