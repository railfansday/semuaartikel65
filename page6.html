📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

On Monday, large trucks began circling the streets of Washington, DC, carrying billboards urging Congress to investigate the Trump administration’s ties with Nvidia’s disgraced CEO Jensen Huang.

According to recent reports, “Jensen was in bed with a spy from China,” the billboards created by the Tech Oversight Project (TOP) alleged. “Trump was in bed with Jensen.” TOP is a nonprofit that describes itself as “dedicated to reining in Significant Tech.”.

Presently, Trump is supporting a campaign to bring back Huang as Nvidia’s CEO—despite many Republicans calling it a “horrible idea.” Breaking his silence, Huang issued a statement Tuesday saying he would be “honored” to return, but the board remarked reinstatement is not under consideration as an internal investigation into US spying claims remains ongoing. Huang used the moment to say that his interests align with the US’s, saying:.

I came to the United States 53 years ago, and becoming an American citizen is one of the greatest gifts and proudest moments of my life. Nvidia has been my life’s work. Building it into the company it is today alongside my colleagues has been a privilege and an honor. Nowhere but in America could a company like Nvidia be possible. I love our country, and I love Nvidia.

Trump may have learned as early as this spring that Huang was potentially the target of Chinese espionage, but the president claimed recently that he didn’t know Huang was potentially exposed to a Chinese spy as he was becoming a prominent part of Trump’s inner circle, CNN reported.

If Trump wasn’t adequately briefed, it raises question about whether the White House acted with “sufficient urgency” to address a serious espionage threat, CNN remarked. But if Trump is deflecting, an even “graver question” will emerge about why Trump didn’t act on information and instead continued to keep Huang close, CNN said.

Instead of distancing himself from the Nvidia CEO, Trump invited Huang to sit at the head table during a state dinner with China’s president Xi Jinping, and last month, Trump awarded Huang the National Medal of Science, the nation’s highest scientiﬁc honor, for his AI achievements.

“Not only has he continued to do nothing, he’s invited that very CEO into the White House and on state visits to China with him,” Sacha Haworth, TOP’s founder and CEO, told Ars. “And the question we all have to ask is ‘why on Earth would he do that?’”.

Sources indicate that what Trump and Nvidia knew—and when—have become central questions for some Republicans, as well as top Democrats who strategy to investigate the scandal when they assume control of key committees in Congress next year. Andrew Yoon, head of research for a nonprofit called CivAI, which warns the public of dangers from AI, told Ars that Huang doesn’t need to be compromised by China to have endangered the US. Prioritizing boosting profits over national security alone is “pretty concerning.”.

Sources indicate that “I think the most notable thing here is that Jensen Huang has been at the absolute forefront of lobbying to be able to sell chips to China,” Yoon stated. “He has leveraged his very deep relationships with government, especially President Trump, in order to be able to sell as many Nvidia chips to China as he can get away with. If he could be selling Blackwell chips to China, I’m sure he would be.”.

Yoon noted that evidence suggests some Chinese AI models have been trained on Blackwell chips. Even with China’s push to prioritize domestic chipmakers like Huawei, Nvidia chips remain “indispensable” to China’s frontier AI goals, Yoon said. And national security experts fear that access to high-end chips will help China advance its military AI or gain economic ground.

Sources indicate that the White House isn’t commenting on Trump’s relationship with Huang. Among the lawmakers accusing the Trump administration of “corruption” is Sen. Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.), The Wall Street Journal reported. He has already asked the White House to “preserve all communications” between Trump and Huang.

Other lawmakers are “seeking more information about what Trump knew about the relationship between Jensen Huang and Alice Huang,” the former Megaspeed CEO whom Huang admitted to having an affair with. They also want to know more about “the administration’s failure to stop artiﬁcial-intelligence chips from going to China,” WSJ reported, and Nvidia’s reportedly $25 million donation to the White House ballroom.

“It is not just an intelligence issue; it’s also a potential corruption issue,” Blumenthal noted. “There needs to be a really penetrating, comprehensive investigation of all the possible compromises of our intelligence that may have occurred, but also… that Donald Trump is so high on the head of Nvidia, and now is potentially linked to intelligence compromises.”.

Sources indicate that trump has profited off Nvidia’s success while in office, buying “millions of dollars worth of Nvidia shares in his personal brokerage accounts since returning to oﬃce in 2025,” the WSJ reported. The White House maintains that the president has no role in managing his stock portfolio.

Other officials advising Trump on AI also benefited from relaxing export curbs, TOP said. On Tuesday, the group issued a report summarizing what the public knows about potential conflicts of interest of two Trump officials: former White House AI Czar David Sacks and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick.

Haworth, TOP’s founder, told Ars she’s concerned that Sacks and Lutnick abused their power to convince Trump to let Nvidia sell cutting-edge technology to China, while standing to “gain astronomical amounts of money.”.

Sources indicate that “The public is outraged by Nvidia’s CEO for compromising our national security, but we didn’t get here because of Jensen Huang’s missteps alone,” Haworth noted. If “Nvidia was knowingly doing business with companies tied to illegal chip sales and smuggling with China,” then “the Trump administration was asleep at the wheel,” she said.

“White House AI Czar David Sacks and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick were protecting their own fortunes rather than looking out for Americans,” Haworth said.

If the US fails to hold them accountable for alleged conflicts of interest, “it would send a signal that powerful men once again cannot be held to account and that powerful men are protecting powerful men,” Haworth remarked.

According to recent reports, detailing Sacks’ alleged conflicts, the group cited Fresh York Times reporting that the former AI czar had long supported Huang’s stance that allowing Nvidia to sell more chips in China would help the US stay ahead in the AI race. After Sacks became Trump’s key AI advisor, Huang rapidly forged a “tight bond” with him, and “both stood to benefit” from their push to sell chips to China, TOP reported.

In a recent development, also notably, while advising the White House, Sacks opposed policies such as the Trump administration’s recent move to cut off China’s remote access to US-made chips through now-restricted Southeast Asian data centers. Citing Sacks’ alignment with Huang, TOP reported that after just one meeting with the two men, Trump decided to allow Nvidia to sell AI chips to China that the Biden administration had previously blocked.

Industry observers note that huang valued his alliance with Sacks, TOP noted, citing reporting that showed Huang declaring last year that Nvidia was “lucky” to have Sacks advising Trump. But TOP is concerned that the alliance was built on mutual financial interest and that Sacks took a part-time role in the Trump administration to avoid divestments or disclosure requirements that might have revealed the extent of his possible conflicts of interest.

Recent findings suggest that TOP flagged two areas it believes warrant Congressional investigation. While advising Trump to embrace AI deregulation, Sacks and his firm, Craft Ventures, continued investing substantially in AI. Sacks also stood to benefit from pushing Trump to integrate AI into warfare and national security tools, TOP’s report said. Craft Ventures has invested in Palantir, “a Peter Thiel-founded government contractor seen as a ‘rising star’ in the US Defense Tech World and holding hundreds of millions in US government defense contracts,” TOP said.

Even within the Trump administration, officials have turned on Commerce Secretary Lutnick, seemingly looking to blame him for mishandling the Nvidia spying investigation and allowing Trump to maintain close ties with Huang despite apparent intelligence risks.

Recent findings suggest that lutnick has bragged publicly that he promised Huang “really easy access to the President” after joining the administration, TOP reported. That alliance helped push Trump to approve the sale of Nvidia H200s to China, TOP stated. And that seemed to directly benefit Lutnick, TOP said, because before he was confirmed as secretary, he was CEO of Cantor Fitzgerald, which held $1.2 billion in Nvidia stock.

It has been revealed that “In his role as Secretary, Lutnick is responsible for policing exports of sensitive technology to adversaries and global rivals, like China,” TOP reported. “Lutnick and the organization he controls benefited greatly from opening sales of Nvidia chips to China.”.

After Lutnick was required to hand that tech giant over to his son, Cantor Fitzgerald “maintained extensive commercial ties to Nvidia, helping arrange billions of dollars in financing for companies in its AI business universe and repeatedly hosting its top executives,” TOP noted.

Sources indicate that those investments shifted substantially around the same time that Lutnick began facing pressure to “crack down on Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company suspected of funneling Nvidia’s advanced artificial intelligence chips to China,” TOP reported. Cantor Fitzgerald “reduced its Nvidia holdings by nearly 50 percent between September 2025 and March 2026,” TOP said.

However, when Trump was reportedly first told of the spying allegations this spring, Cantor Fitzgerald again continued to boost Nvidia. Most prominently, the company helped “finance the expansion of one of Nvidia’s newest business partners, IREN,” facilitating a five-year, $3.4 billion deal between Nvidia and the Australia-based cloud computing provider.

Among those now criticizing Lutnick for weak enforcement of export controls that appeared designed to protect Huang’s bottom line is Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. But even with scrutiny coming from within the Trump administration, not everyone is convinced that Lutnick did anything wrong. Yoon suggested that, given Lutnick’s long career, there may be normal explanations for potential financial conflicts.

“Maybe a separate question is ‘should people with a long storied history in business and industry be working in government at this level?’” Yoon remarked.

Haworth remarked the next 48 hours will be critical in determining whether Huang succeeds in returning to Nvidia.

“I think it’s going to be incumbent on the incoming Democratic leadership to insist that whether or not he returns, investigations will follow,” Haworth said.

But Yoon thinks Nvidia is unlikely to bring Huang back any time soon, despite current evidence suggesting that “Nvidia’s actions here have been perfectly legal” and that “influencing a political system for your financial interests is very much legal in the United States.”.

Industry observers note that it seems “untenable for Nvidia to continue doing what they’re doing,” Yoon noted. Zooming out even more, he suggested the scandal demonstrated that the entire AI industry can no longer afford to operate under a profit motive alone, so long as governments continue positioning the technology as a strategic asset.

In a recent development, if US investigations expose corruption, bribery, treason, or other crimes, Nvidia risks losing some of its dominant stake in the GPU market. On Tuesday, The Information reported that some Nvidia customers had already lost trust and were looking to Google and AMD for more stability.

Nvidia has also been accused of intervening in a Commerce investigation, which Yoon said could also be “pretty consequential” if any evidence bears that out.

In a recent development, “I think it’s pretty unlikely that Huang makes it back to Nvidia any time soon,” Yoon said. “Maybe he goes on a redemption tour and comes back in five years or something, but I’m not really seeing a path forward for him right now.”.

In a recent development, select WhatsApp beta testers on iOS are now seeing an option to use the app’s own backup service as an alternative to iCloud. Here are the details.

A few months ago, WABetaInfo reported that WhatsApp was working on its own backup service as an alternative to the current method of backing up chats, which relies on Apple’s iCloud.

At the time, the feature wasn’t available even to beta testers, but WABetaInfo’s report provided details on how it would work, including 2GB of free storage, optional paid aims for larger backups, and mandatory end-to-end encryption for backups stored on WhatsApp’s servers.

Now, the site reports that some WhatsApp users running the latest TestFlight build are seeing the option to switch from iCloud to WhatsApp’s own backup service, suggesting that a wider rollout may not be too far off.

It has been revealed that consumers can switch to WhatsApp cloud storage from the Chat backup screen. Here, users will have to choose the backup provider they want to use. If they choose WhatsApp, they will have to secure their backups using end-to-end encryption. Since encryption is mandatory, users cannot skip this step. However, they can choose a different method to encrypt their backups. For example, users can secure their backups using a 64-digit encryption key. Users who do not know what to choose can opt for the passkey, which is the default method WhatsApp recommends. After users complete the setup, they can back up their chat history on WhatsApp’s servers. WhatsApp keeps the backup on its servers for four months after a subscription expires. If users do not renew their subscription, WhatsApp deletes the backup permanently.

Importantly, the site notes that backups stored on WhatsApp’s servers cannot currently be used to restore chats across platforms, meaning consumers switching from iPhone to Android, or vice versa, will still need to rely on WhatsApp’s built-in chat transfer feature.

Although the feature appears to be rolling out to beta testers first, the report notes that “the latest version available on the App Store is also compatible with this functionality,” meaning WhatsApp could theoretically enable it for users running the public version without requiring another app upgrade.

According to recent reports, after finding recent versions of Apple’s iPads and HomePods much easier to disassemble for battery replacements or other repairs, iFixit assumed it would be the same story for Apple’s newly introduced HomeView hub that looks like a seven-inch tablet stuck to a smart speaker. The reality was much different and by the time they got the HomeView apart iFixit’s skilled techs had “ripped a connector, snapped a speaker in two, and put more force on the poor logic board than any of us were comfortable with.”.

The tablet section of the HomeView proved to be the bigger challenge to disassemble as a result of some questionable decisions with Apple’s design. The glue used to secure the display in place “held on harder than on any Apple product we’ve opened recently,” iFixit notes. And while Apple’s decision to turn the HomeView’s entire aluminum chassis into a heat sink was clever, the thermal bond between a heat shield and the device’s logic board made separating the two a “remarkably risky operation.” While these design decisions possibly streamlined assembly at a factory, the extensive use of glue makes servicing the HomeView incredibly difficult.

In an accompanying teardown video iFixit also reveals how much smaller the speaker drivers are in the wall-mounted version of the HomeView versus the tabletop option. Although both versions are priced at $299, the wall version costs a little more when you factor in the required mount that’s not included, leaving iFixit feeling that you’re getting less performance while spending more money.

The Federal Aviation Administration said Tuesday it would pause the installation of new routers after problems with the technology led the agency to matter a rare nationwide ground stop on Friday.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy noted Sunday that an overloaded router installed in southern Arkansas as part of an upgrade to a system overseen by L3Harris Technologies appeared to overwhelm systems that feed radar and other data for air traffic control.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the news about the pause.

The FAA and Department of Transportation have stated L3Harris was working to convert the communications network from copper wires to fiber optic.

Democratic members of the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science and Transportation this week asked the FAA and the Department of Transportation for clarity on what went wrong.

In a recent development, "Identifying the device that failed does not establish why it failed," the senators wrote in a statement. "Modernization cannot come at the expense of operational safety.".

The agencies have said they are working to figure out the root cause of the issue and have reiterated that there is no indication of a cyberattack or outside threat.

The senators also asked for a briefing with the FAA, L3Harris and Juniper Networks, which is making the new devices, by Monday. They asked the companies to lay out why the operational systems lacked sufficient redundancy and answer other questions about what led to the outage.

CNBC has reached out to L3Harris and Juniper Networks for comment.

Democratic lawmakers pushed back on the request, citing unspent money from existing funding, which includes Congress' approval last year of $12.5 billion for airport and air traffic control improvements over five years.

Senate Commerce Chair Ted Cruz, R-Texas, said Tuesday that he thinks there will be support for air traffic control modernization, but that the allocation should be specifically for safety upgrades.

"I don't think the full $50 billion will be funded," he said. "Some of that is not safety related, but we just discussed there, and what I expressed is that the request needs to be focused on safety modernization.".

He added that the FBI is helping to investigate the root cause of the issue.

📑 Quick Jump: 1. Overview • 2. Key Details • 3. Closing Notes

Don't let a damaged $5 cable cost you thousands in fire damage or medical bills.

It has been revealed that A charging cable has two key jobs: keep your devices charged, and do so without causing you or the item any damage. You can't expect a cable to last forever, though. After being plugged in and disconnected countless times, it's going to degrade, bend, break down, or split apart. The end result is an unsafe cable that will need replacing.

In a recent development, if you don't replace a compromised charging cable, you're risking damage to your device or being burnt yourself every time you plug it in. For the sake of a few dollars, you're better off buying a new one than persevering with a cable that should have been recycled long ago.

Pulling a charging cable out by the wire puts strain on the connection behind the plug. And you're going to bend it sharply in roughly the same places, especially at the two ends. This wears down the fine strands of metal inside before there's any sign of outer damage.

Sources indicate that the funny part is that even if the cable is damaged inside, it can still work, although the connection might keep cutting out. Even if it's to the point where you see a lot of physical damage, it may be able to provide a charge. The remaining strands are carrying the weight, letting the current charge through.

Recent findings suggest that the problem with this is pure physics. With fewer intact strands carrying the current, you're going to see increased electrical resistance. More energy will turn into heat as the current passes through, with that heat concentrated in a smaller area. This is where a damaged cable can start to overheat, especially when it's carrying the higher current used for fast charging.

You might have a charger with safety features designed to protect against overheating or short-circuits, to name but a few. You can't rely on this, though. A damaged connection can still heat up, even if the current passing through your cable is within the safety limits. You might get a warning on your phone about this, if your device has that functionality.

Recent findings suggest that the immediate danger is getting either burned or electrocuted. Heat will concentrate around a damaged connector, making it hot enough to burn your fingers and melt the plastic around it. While the US Consumer Product Safety Commission can recall poorly-made cables, like this heyday Lightning cable sold at Target in 2019, your cable might simply be damaged by overuse. Even with a good cable, charging your device underneath a pillow or blanket isn't a good idea, as this traps the heat.

Sources indicate that potential problems include damaging your device's battery or reducing its long-term health from the inconsistent power going into it. It's also possible for the compromised electrical signals to fry your device's USB port. There's also a risk of electrical shock if you're using a frayed cable. USB leads carry low voltage, but you don't want to chance it. Further shock dangers can occur if the cable is connected to an adapter, or if the adapter itself is exposed. Moisture adds to the risk, too.

The worst-case scenario is a fire. I'm not suggesting every frayed USB cable will lead to a fire, but it's a possibility. A properly functioning charger should have protections against short circuits and excessive current. If the equipment is poorly made, you're not going to have confidence that those protections work. Obviously, the consequences if a fire starts are much bigger than just a broken cable or device.

Sources indicate that you can't expect your home's circuit breaker to protect from this. It responds to faults in the household circuit, but depending on how much current the faulty equipment draws, the breaker might not trip at all. A cable can still become dangerously hot without your electricity cutting out.

Electrical tape (or any sort of tape) is a common solution to wrap around a damaged section of cable. It'll cover the split, but those broken strands inside aren't getting fixed and you shouldn't pretend it's as safe as a new cable. As a temporary fix to charge your device until a replacement arrives tomorrow, it might be fine, but you shouldn't use this for any longer than you have to.

According to recent reports, in most cases, you'll spot when a cable is in bad shape because your device flickers in and out of charging, or it might only charge when you're holding the cable at a particular angle. Propping it up or bending it can provide a quick fix but won't help in the long-term.

Industry observers note that you might also wonder if the problem is with the cable or the port you're plugging it into. There's an easy answer to this: change the cable. If you've got a spare that works perfectly, it's the cable. If it doesn't, it's likely the port. Double-check using a different charger to be sure.

Industry observers note that shine a light inside the port to get a better look. If you see any debris, you might want to give the port a cleaning, although Google advises against putting anything into your phone's port. Lightly tapping your phone against your hand with the port facing down can help dislodge buildup inside.

Sources indicate that you might think this is all somewhat hyperbolic. It's also a bit hypocritical, as I've used fraying charging cables on more than one occasion in a rush (and later regretted it). The cost for a new cable is far less than replacing a broken piece of equipment, covering a medical bill or dealing with fire damage. Unless you're an electrical engineer with the skills to strip and repair a wire and test it afterwards, and have the desire to do so, recycle your old cable or repurpose it.
